# Lingshu, Chapter 28 — 口問篇第二十八

**English translation for the series.** *Kǒu Wèn* — "Oral Inquiry": the questions
the Emperor asked after he had sent everyone else out of the room.

> A dramatized adaptation of a classical philosophical text. Not medical advice.
> Nothing below is a diagnosis, a remedy or a protocol, and nothing below should
> be read as one. The chapter closes each answer with a needling prescription;
> those clauses are translated here for completeness, marked as such, and are
> **excluded from the episode entirely**.

## About this translation

The source is the received classical text of this chapter, compiled around the
Han period and long in the **public domain**. **This English rendering is
original work made for this series**, not a reproduction of any published
translation.

**Provenance.** The user supplied the chapter as a **PDF** (a ctext-style
bilingual page, captured 2026-07-08, 5 pp.), and the 原文 below is transcribed
from it. `ctext.org`, `zh.wikisource.org` and `8bei8.com` are all **unreachable
from the repo host** — the agent proxy denies them at CONNECT — so a PDF supplied
by hand is now the standing route into this repo, as recorded in
`inner-canon-ch1-translation-v4.md` and `inner-canon-ch6-translation-v1.md`.

The PDF also carries a facing English translation. **It was read and then set
aside**: the rendering below is made from the Chinese, and where the two differ
the difference is recorded in *Four places the supplied English goes wrong*
rather than silently absorbed. Three of those four are not stylistic — they
change which bodily act is being discussed.

The PDF is in **simplified** characters; the text below is given in
**traditional**, matching the rest of `output/`. The conversion touches
orthography only. Two cases are worth naming:

- The source writes the physician's name **歧伯**. Most received editions print
  **岐伯**, and the PDF's own English romanises it *Qi Bo*. Both forms are found;
  the transcription keeps the source's 歧伯 and the English uses **Qi Bo**
  throughout.
- The source writes **寫** where later editions print **瀉** (*to drain*, the
  counterpart of 補 *to supplement*). 寫 is the older orthography for the same
  word, not a different instruction.

### ⚠ The chapter number needs qualifying on screen

This is **Lingshu 28** — the twenty-eighth chapter of the 靈樞, the *Spiritual
Pivot*, the second half of the Inner Canon. It is **not Su Wen 28**, which is a
different chapter entirely (通評虛實論, on assessing depletion and repletion).

The two halves of the canon are numbered separately and both run to eighty-one
chapters, so **an unqualified "Chapter 28" on screen is ambiguous by
construction** and points a checking viewer at the wrong text roughly half the
time. **Cite it on screen as *Lingshu 28*, or as *Spiritual Pivot, chapter
28*.** Keep `ch28` for the folder and the file names, which follow the input
script (`Chapt_LS28__why_we_yawn.pdf`).

### Style

Deliberately **plain rather than literary**. The series' arguments turn on words
that smoother translations often smooth away — 口傳 *"handed on by mouth"*,
奇邪 *"strange qi"*, 皆為不足 *"in every case, something is missing"* — so those
are rendered explicitly even where it costs elegance.

The chapter is a catalogue of small involuntary bodily acts, and **each one is
named by a single Chinese character that English has no single word for**. Where
that happens the character is kept, transliterated, and glossed on first use:
欠 *qiàn*, 噦 *yuě*, 唏 *xī*, 噫 *ài*, 嚏 *tì*, 嚲 *duǒ*. Getting these wrong
collapses two riddles into one, which is exactly what the supplied English does
twice.

Technical terms are left transliterated where English has no honest equivalent
(*qi*, *yin/yang*, *zang/fu*, *shaoyin*, *taiyang*) and glossed on first use.
Bracketed words are supplied for sense and are not in the Chinese.

**Needling prescriptions** are set off as `**[Needling — not used in the
episode]**`. They are translated because a translation that silently drops a
clause is not a translation. They do not appear in the longform script, the
trailer, or any on-screen card.

## 1. The Emperor clears the room

> 黃帝閒居，辟左右而問於歧伯曰：余已聞九針之經，論陰陽逆順，六經已畢，願得口問。

*The Yellow Emperor, at leisure, **dismissed his attendants** and asked Qi Bo:
"I have already heard the canon of the nine needles; I have discussed yin and
yang, contrary and compliant; I have finished with the six channels. Now I
should like to ask **by mouth**."*

> 歧伯避席再拜曰：善乎哉問也，此先師之所口傳也。

*Qi Bo **rose from his mat and bowed twice**, and said: "What a fine question.
This is what my former teachers handed on **by mouth**."*

> 黃帝曰：願聞口傳。

*The Yellow Emperor said: "I should like to hear what was handed on by mouth."*

> 歧伯答曰：夫百病之始生也，皆生於風雨寒暑，陰陽喜怒，飲食居處，大驚卒恐。

*Qi Bo replied: "The hundred diseases, at their beginning, all arise from wind
and rain, cold and heat; from yin and yang, joy and anger; from food and drink
and the conditions one lives in; from great alarm and sudden terror."*

> 則血氣分離，陰陽破敗，經絡厥絕，脈道不通，陰陽相逆，衛氣稽留，經脈虛空，血氣不次，乃失其常。

*"Then blood and qi come apart; yin and yang are broken; the channels and
collaterals are cut off and reversed; the roads of the vessels do not open; yin
and yang run counter to one another; **defensive qi is held up in transit**; the
channel vessels stand empty; blood and qi lose their order — and so the body
loses its constancy."*

> 論不在經者，請道其方。

*"**As for what is not in the classics** — allow me to set out the method of
it."*

**Note.** Two moves are made here, and everything after depends on both. First,
辟左右 — *he sends the attendants away*. Nothing that follows is a state secret;
what follows are undignified questions, and the Emperor arranges not to be
overheard asking them. Second, 論不在經者 — *what is not in the classics*. The
chapter announces itself as material that lies **outside the written canon**,
handed on 口傳, teacher to student, by mouth. It then proceeds to be written
down, which is the chapter's quiet joke on itself and the spine of the episode.

The list of causes — weather, emotion, diet, living conditions, sudden fright —
is deliberately ordinary. Nothing exotic is invoked, and no agent outside the
world is invoked at all.

## 2. 欠 — the yawn

> 黃帝曰：人之欠者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **yawns** — what qi brings it about?"*

> 歧伯答曰：衛氣晝日行於陽，夜半則行於陰，陰者主夜，夜者臥；陽者主上，陰者主下；

*Qi Bo replied: "Defensive qi travels in the yang through the daylight, and at
midnight it travels in the yin. Yin governs the night, and night is for lying
down; yang governs what is above, yin governs what is below."*

> 故陰氣積於下，陽氣未盡，陽引而上，陰引而下，陰陽相引，故數欠。

*"So yin qi gathers below while yang qi is not yet spent. **Yang draws upward,
yin draws downward, the two pull against one another — and therefore one yawns,
and yawns again.**"*

> 陽氣盡，陰氣盛，則目瞑；陰氣盡而陽氣盛，則寤矣。

*"When yang qi is spent and yin qi is full, the eyes close. When yin qi is spent
and yang qi is full, one wakes."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 寫足少陰，補足太陽。
> *Drain foot shaoyin; supplement foot taiyang.*

**Note.** This is the chapter's crown jewel and the reason it was chosen. The
answer is a **tug of war**: two forces pulling in opposite directions at the same
moment, with the yawn as the visible tremor in the rope. And it does not stop at
the yawn — the same sentence, continued, produces falling asleep and waking up.
**One mechanism, three phenomena.** That is what a theory is, and it is why this
chapter is history of science rather than folklore.

It is also, as physiology, wrong. What it gets right is *when*: yawning does
cluster at the hinge between sleep and waking, which is the single most robust
descriptive finding about yawning in the modern literature. Right about the
timing, wrong about the cause — a distinction the episode returns to repeatedly.

## 3. 噦 — the hiccup

> 黃帝曰：人之噦者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **hiccups** — what qi brings it
about?"*

> 歧伯曰：穀入於胃，胃氣上注於肺。今有故寒氣與新穀氣，俱還入於胃，新故相亂，真邪相攻，氣並相逆，復出於胃，故為噦。

*Qi Bo said: "Grain enters the stomach, and stomach qi pours upward into the
lung. Now if **old cold qi** and **newly arrived grain qi** both return into the
stomach together, new and old fall into disorder; the true and the aberrant
attack one another; the qi combine and run counter; it comes back out of the
stomach — and so there is a hiccup."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補手太陰，寫足少陰。
> *Supplement hand taiyin; drain foot shaoyin.*

**Note.** 噦 is the hiccup — the involuntary catch with the sound made on the
intake. The supplied English renders it *"sighs"*, which is wrong and collides
with §10, where 太息, the long deliberate sigh, is a separate riddle with a
separate answer. Two of the twelve become one if this is not fixed.

The answer's grammar is worth watching, because eight of the twelve share it:
**something arrives, something reverses, and it leaves by the way it came in.**

## 4. 唏 — the catching sob

> 黃帝曰：人之唏者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sobs — the shuddering catch of breath
that comes after weeping** — what qi brings it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：此陰氣盛而陽氣虛，陰氣疾而陽氣徐，陰氣盛而陽氣絕，故為唏。

*Qi Bo said: "This is yin qi full while yang qi is depleted; yin qi quick while
yang qi is slow; yin qi flooding until yang qi is cut off — and so there is the
catching sob."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陽，寫足少陰。
> *Supplement foot taiyang; drain foot shaoyin.*

**Note — a genuine dispute, and we take a side.** 唏 has two readings in the
commentarial tradition. The mainstream one, and the one taken here, reads it as
歔欷 — **the involuntary shuddering intake of breath that follows crying**. The
minority reading, which the supplied English follows, makes it *shivering and
teeth-chattering*.

The minority reading is rejected here on internal grounds: **§5 is 振寒, which
already is shivering**, with its own separate answer. Reading §4 the same way
produces two consecutive riddles about the same act with two different
explanations, and leaves the canon's most conspicuous emotional reflex — the
sob — absent from a chapter that otherwise catalogues every involuntary noise a
body makes. The episode states the dispute on screen rather than hiding it.

## 5. 振寒 — the shiver

> 黃帝曰：人之振寒者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **shivers with cold** — what qi brings
it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：寒氣客於皮膚，陰氣盛，陽氣虛，故為振寒寒慄，補諸陽。

*Qi Bo said: "Cold qi **lodges as a guest** in the skin; yin qi is full and yang
qi depleted — and so there is shivering and trembling with cold."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補諸陽。
> *Supplement all the yang [channels].*

**Note.** The shortest answer in the chapter: one sentence, no cascade, no
staging. 客 — *lodges as a guest* — is the standard verb for a pathogen taking up
residence in the body, and it is doing real work: the cold is not part of you, it
is **staying** with you. The episode keeps the guest metaphor because it is the
chapter's own.

## 6. 噫 — the belch

> 黃帝曰：人之噫者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **belches** — what qi brings it
about?"*

> 歧伯曰：寒氣客於胃，厥逆從下上散，復出於胃，故為噫。

*Qi Bo said: "Cold qi lodges as a guest in the stomach; it turns back and
disperses upward from below; it comes back out of the stomach — and so there is
a belch."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陰陽明，一曰補眉本也。
> *Supplement foot taiyin and yangming. **Another says**: supplement at the root
> of the eyebrow.*

**Note — 一曰, "another says."** This clause is the most interesting three
characters in the chapter and the episode gives them a block. The text records a
**second, incompatible opinion** and does not adjudicate between them. It appears
again in §7 (*another says: above the eyebrow*) and in §15 (*another says: below
the outer ankle*).

That is what an oral tradition looks like when it is finally written down: two
teachers said two things, the student who wrote it down had heard both, and he
kept both. A chapter that opened by announcing it was **not in the classics**
preserves its own disagreements — which is precisely the evidence that it really
was transmitted the way it says it was.

## 7. 嚏 — the sneeze

> 黃帝曰：人之嚏者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sneezes** — what qi brings it
about?"*

> 歧伯曰：陽氣和利，滿於心，出於鼻，故為嚏。

*Qi Bo said: "**Yang qi is harmonious and running freely**; it fills the heart;
it issues from the nose — and so there is a sneeze."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陽榮眉本，一曰眉上也。
> *Supplement the foot taiyang [channel] at the root of the eyebrow. Another
> says: above the eyebrow.*

**Note.** The odd one out, and the episode makes a point of it. Every other
answer in the chapter is a story about **deficiency, disorder or cold** — §14
will say so outright. The sneeze alone is explained by **things going well**: qi
harmonious, running freely, abundant enough to overflow. It is the only riddle in
the chapter whose answer is that the body is working.

---

## 8. 嚲 — limpness

> 黃帝曰：人之嚲者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **body goes slack — the limb that
hangs heavy and will not answer** — what qi brings it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：胃不實則諸脈虛；諸脈虛則筋脈懈惰；筋脈懈惰則行陰用力，氣不能復，故為嚲。

*Qi Bo said: "If the stomach is not full, all the vessels are empty. If all the
vessels are empty, the sinews and vessels go slack and idle. If the sinews and
vessels are slack and idle, then [when] one **exerts oneself in the yin**, the qi
cannot be restored — and so there is limpness."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 因其所在，補分肉間。
> *According to where it is, supplement between the flesh divisions.*

**Note — two corrections.** First, **嚲 is not drooling**. It means *drooping,
hanging slack*; here, the limbs going heavy and unresponsive. The supplied
English renders it *"drools"*, which collides with §11, where 涎下 — saliva
running down — is a separate riddle with a separate answer. This is the second of
the two collisions that would collapse the twelve into ten.

Second, **行陰用力** is ambiguous and the reading matters. It can mean *exerting
oneself in the yin [parts, i.e. moving the lower body]*, and it can mean
*sexual exertion*; commentators have taken both. The neutral reading is used
here and in the episode, on the same principle applied to 作強 in
`inner-canon-ch6-translation-v1.md`: where two readings are defensible, take the
one the words best support and do not stage the other.

This is also the most machine-like answer in the chapter — a four-link chain
where each link is stated as a conditional. Empty stomach → empty vessels →
slack sinews → exertion that cannot be paid back.

## 9. 泣涕 — weeping

> 黃帝曰：人之哀而泣涕出者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **grieves, and tears and nasal
discharge come out** — what qi brings it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：心者，五藏六府之主也；目者，宗脈之所聚也，上液之道也；口鼻者，氣之門戶也。

*Qi Bo said: "**The heart is the ruler of the five zang and the six fu.** The
eyes are where the **great converging vessels** gather, and they are the road by
which fluid ascends. **The mouth and nose are the gates through which qi passes
in and out.**"*

> 故悲哀愁憂則心動，心動則五藏六府皆搖，搖則宗脈感，宗脈感則液道開，液道開，故泣涕出焉。

*"So when there is sorrow, grief, care and worry, **the heart is moved**. When
the heart is moved, all five zang and six fu are **shaken**. When they are
shaken, the great converging vessels **respond**. When the vessels respond, the
fluid road **opens**. And the road being open — the tears and the discharge come
out."*

> 液者，所以灌精濡空竅者也，故上液之道開則泣，泣不止則液竭；

*"Fluid is what irrigates the essence and moistens the hollow orifices. So when
the upward road of fluid opens, one weeps; and if the weeping does not stop, the
fluid is exhausted."*

> 液竭則精不灌，精不灌則目無所見矣，故命曰奪精。

*"When the fluid is exhausted, the essence is not irrigated; when the essence is
not irrigated, **the eyes see nothing**. And so this is named **奪精 — the
robbing of the essence**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補天柱經俠頸。
> *Supplement the Tianzhu channel, flanking the neck.*

**Note.** The longest and best-built answer in the chapter, and the episode's
emotional centre. It is a **chain of custody for a single tear**: grief → the
heart moves → every organ shakes → the vessels answer → a road opens → water
comes out of your face. Every link is named, in order, and the chain runs from an
emotion to a fluid without ever leaving the body.

**The closing claim is false and the episode says so, plainly and immediately.**
Weeping does not exhaust a finite reservoir, and crying does not take your
eyesight. 奪精 is a beautiful phrase attached to a wrong mechanism, and the
series' rule is to say which is which rather than let the phrase carry the claim.

## 10. 太息 — the long sigh

> 黃帝曰：人之太息者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sighs — the long breath drawn and let
go** — what qi brings it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：憂思則心系急，心系急則氣道約，約則不利，故太息以伸出之，

*Qi Bo said: "Worry and brooding make **the cords of the heart draw tight**. When
the cords of the heart are tight, the road of qi is **constricted**. Constricted,
it does not run freely — and so one sighs, **to stretch it open and let it
out**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補手少陰心主、足少陽留之也。
> *Supplement hand shaoyin and the heart governor, and foot shaoyang; retain
> [the needle].*

**Note.** The chapter's best score against the modern account, and the episode
gives it the scorecard's top line. Two claims are made: that sighing keeps
company with worry, and that its purpose is to **open something that has become
constricted**.

The first is correct as an association — sighing does increase with anxiety.
The second is closer to right than anything else in the chapter: the physiological
job of a spontaneous sigh is to reinflate parts of the lung that have collapsed
and restore its compliance, and there is a dedicated brainstem circuit that
generates them. **Constriction, and a breath that opens it, is the right shape.**
The organ is wrong — it is the lung, not the cords of the heart — and the episode
says so rather than rounding the near-miss up to a hit.

## 11. 涎下 — drooling

> 黃帝曰：人之涎下者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **saliva runs down** — what qi brings
it about?"*

> 歧伯曰：飲食者，皆入於胃，胃中有熱則蟲動，蟲動則胃緩，胃緩則廉泉開，故涎下，

*Qi Bo said: "Food and drink all enter the stomach. When there is **heat in the
stomach, the worms stir**. When the worms stir, the stomach **slackens**. When
the stomach slackens, **Lianquan opens** — and so the saliva runs down."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足少陰。
> *Supplement foot shaoyin.*

**Note.** 蟲 — worms — is meant literally. Intestinal parasites were a routine
fact of life and the text reaches for them the way it reaches for cold and wind:
as an ordinary cause, not a marvel. The episode keeps the worms in, because a
mechanism that is specific, physical, checkable **and wrong** is more instructive
than a vague one that cannot be wrong at all.

廉泉 *Lianquan* — "the modest spring" — names the point beneath the tongue where
saliva was held to arise, conventionally identified with the point later
standardised as CV23. The chapter treats it as a valve.

## 12. 耳鳴 — ringing in the ears

> 黃帝曰：人之耳中鳴者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **ears ring** — what qi brings it
about?"*

> 歧伯曰：耳者，宗脈之所聚也，故胃中空則宗脈虛，虛則下溜，脈有所竭者，故耳鳴，

*Qi Bo said: "The ears are where the **great converging vessels** gather. So when
the stomach is empty, the converging vessels are depleted; being depleted, they
**drain downward**; and where a vessel has run dry — the ear rings."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補客主人，手大指爪甲上與肉交者也。
> *Supplement Kezhuren, and [the point] on the thumb where the nail meets the
> flesh.*

**Note — the supplied English merges two points into one.** It reads *"Kezhuoren
(TE21), which is located at the junction of the flesh and nail bed on the thumb"*
— but 客主人 *Kezhuren* is a point in front of the ear (the classical name for
the point later standardised as GB3), and 手大指爪甲上與肉交者 is a **second,
separate** point at the corner of the thumbnail (later standardised as LU11). One
is beside the ear and one is on the hand; they are not the same place. Recorded
for accuracy, and moot for the episode, which uses no needling material at all.

The riddle itself pairs with §9: **the ears and the eyes are both places where
the 宗脈 converge.** That single structural claim is doing double duty — it
explains tears in one chapter-section and tinnitus in another, from the same
premise. It is the chapter's system working as designed, and it is wrong in both
places.

## 13. 自齧舌 — biting your own tongue

> 黃帝曰：人之自齧舌者，何氣使然？此厥逆走上，脈氣輩至也。

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **bites his own tongue** — what qi
brings it about?" [Qi Bo said:] "This is qi turning back and **running upward**,
the vessel-qi **arriving out of turn**."*

> 少陰氣至則齧舌，少陽氣至則齧頰，陽明氣至則齧唇矣。

*"When shaoyin qi arrives, one bites the **tongue**. When shaoyang qi arrives,
one bites the **cheek**. When yangming qi arrives, one bites the **lip**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 視主病者，則補之。
> *See which [channel] governs the trouble, and supplement that one.*

**Note.** The strangest riddle and the best-shaped wrong answer in the chapter.
It is wrong — biting your tongue is a motor-timing error in the coordination of
jaw and tongue, and has nothing to do with channels. But look at the *form* of
the answer: 輩至, **arriving out of turn**. The text's explanation for an
involuntary self-injury is a **scheduling failure** — something got somewhere at
the wrong moment.

The episode is careful here not to round that up into a hit. Being the right
shape of answer is not the same as being right, and the chapter's own three-way
mapping — tongue, cheek, lip, each assigned to a different channel — is a
specific prediction that does not hold.

## 14. 凡此十二邪 — all these twelve

> 凡此十二邪者，皆奇邪之走空竅者也。故邪之所在，皆為不足。

*"**All these twelve** are **strange qi running into the hollow openings**. And
so, wherever the aberrant qi is — **in every case, something is missing.**"*

> 故上氣不足，腦為之不滿，耳為之苦鳴，頭為之苦傾，目為之眩。

*"So when the qi above is insufficient, the brain is not filled, the ears suffer
ringing, the head suffers a tendency to tip forward, and the eyes swim."*

> 中氣不足，溲便為之變，腸為之苦鳴。

*"When the qi of the centre is insufficient, the passing of water and stool is
altered, and the intestines suffer rumbling."*

> 下氣不足，則乃為痿厥心悗。

*"When the qi below is insufficient, there is withering and cold reversal in the
limbs, and oppression at the heart."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足外踝下留之。
> *Supplement below the outer ankle and retain [the needle].*

**Note — this is the sentence the whole chapter has been building to.** 皆為不足
— *in every case, something is missing.* Twelve phenomena, twelve separate
answers, and then a single line claiming they all have one thing in common: a
**deficit**. Not an excess, not an invader with its own agenda. Something that
should be there is not.

That is a real theoretical move, and it is the moment the anthology stops being
an anthology. It is also the moment to notice what it costs: 嚏, the sneeze, was
explained in §7 by qi being **harmonious and abundant** — the one riddle whose
answer is that things are going well. §14 sweeps it in with the rest anyway.
**The unifying claim does not actually fit all twelve, and the chapter does not
notice.** The episode does.

**And the count is exact.** 十二 — twelve — is not a round number here. Counting
the riddles as they are asked: 欠 yawn, 噦 hiccup, 唏 sob, 振寒 shiver, 噫 belch,
嚏 sneeze, 嚲 limpness, 泣涕 weeping, 太息 sigh, 涎下 drooling, 耳鳴 tinnitus,
自齧舌 tongue-biting. Twelve asked, twelve answered, twelve recapitulated in §15.
The tally survives a viewer counting along — **provided §3 and §8 are translated
correctly**, which is exactly what the two collisions in the supplied English
would destroy.

## 15. How they are treated

> 黃帝曰：治之奈何？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "How are these treated?"*

> **[Needling — the whole of this section is needling prescription, and none of
> it is used in the episode. Translated for completeness only.]**

> 歧伯曰：腎主為欠，取足少陰；肺主為噦，取手太陰、足少陰；唏者，陰與陽絕，故補足太陽，寫足少陰；振寒者，補諸陽；噫者，補足太陰陽明；嚏者，補足太陽眉本；嚲，因其所在，補分肉間；泣出補天柱經俠頸，俠頸者，頭中分也；太息，補手少陰、心主、足少陽，留之；涎下補足少陰；耳鳴補客主人，手大指爪甲上與肉交者；自齧舌，視主病者，則補之。

*Qi Bo said: "The kidney governs yawning — take foot shaoyin. The lung governs
hiccup — take hand taiyin and foot shaoyin. For the catching sob, yin and yang
are cut off from one another, so supplement foot taiyang and drain foot shaoyin.
For shivering with cold, supplement all the yang. For belching, supplement foot
taiyin and yangming. For sneezing, supplement foot taiyang at the root of the
eyebrow. For limpness, according to where it is, supplement between the flesh
divisions. For weeping, supplement the Tianzhu channel flanking the neck —
'flanking the neck' means the midline of the head. For the long sigh, supplement
hand shaoyin, the heart governor, and foot shaoyang, and retain [the needle]. For
saliva running down, supplement foot shaoyin. For ringing in the ears, supplement
Kezhuren, and [the point] on the thumb where the nail meets the flesh. For biting
one's own tongue, see which [channel] governs the trouble and supplement that
one."*

> 目眩頭傾，補足外踝下留之；痿厥心悗，刺足大趾間上二寸，留之，一曰足外踝下留之。

*"For swimming eyes and a head that tips forward, supplement below the outer
ankle and retain. For withering, cold reversal and oppression at the heart,
needle two cun above the cleft between the great toes and retain — **another
says**: below the outer ankle, and retain."*

**Note.** A recapitulation, in the order the riddles were asked, with two new
attributions that were not in the answers themselves: **the kidney governs
yawning, the lung governs hiccup.** Neither appears in §2 or §3. The chapter is
reconciling itself with the organ system used elsewhere in the canon, after the
fact — which is what a compiled text looks like at the seams.

And it closes on **一曰** one final time. Even the last line of the chapter
records a second opinion.

## Four places the supplied English goes wrong

Recorded because three of the four change **which bodily act is being
discussed**, and any of the three would break the count of twelve on screen.

| # | Chinese | Supplied English | This translation | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | §3 噦 | "sighs" | **hiccup** | Collides with §10 太息, the actual sigh. Two riddles become one. |
| B | §8 嚲 | "drools" | **limpness / slackness** | Collides with §11 涎下, the actual drooling. Two riddles become one. |
| C | §12 客主人 + 手大指爪甲上 | one point, "Kezhuoren (TE21) … on the thumb" | **two separate points** — one before the ear, one at the thumbnail | Merges two locations into an impossible one. Moot for the episode; recorded for accuracy. |
| D | §4 唏 | "shivers or chatters their teeth" | **the catching sob** | Defensible minority reading, but it duplicates §5 振寒. See §4's note; the episode states the dispute rather than concealing it. |

A and B together would reduce the chapter's twelve riddles to ten while §14 still
says 十二. **Anything on screen that counts must be built from this file, not
from the supplied English.**

## Reading notes for production

Six things the series treats as load-bearing, all recoverable from the Chinese
above:

1. **The frame is the content.** 辟左右 — he clears the room — and 論不在經者 —
   *what is not in the classics*. The chapter is explicitly the stuff too small,
   too undignified or too unofficial to write down; and it was written down. The
   most formal gesture in the chapter (Qi Bo rising off his mat and bowing twice,
   避席再拜) is triggered by the least dignified question in the canon.

2. **One mechanism, three phenomena.** §2 explains yawning, falling asleep and
   waking with a single sentence about defensive qi. That is the difference
   between a theory and a list, and it is the strongest claim the episode makes
   for taking the chapter seriously.

3. **Right about when, wrong about why.** Yawning really does cluster at the
   sleep–wake hinge (§2); sighing really does keep company with worry (§10); a
   full stomach really is a hiccup trigger (§3). The observations are good and
   the mechanisms are wrong. **These are separate scores and the episode never
   merges them.**

4. **The chapter preserves its own disagreements.** 一曰 — *another says* —
   appears in §6, §7 and §15. A text that announced itself as oral transmission
   carries the fingerprints of oral transmission: two teachers, two answers, both
   kept, neither adjudicated.

5. **皆為不足 does not actually fit all twelve.** §14's unifying claim — wherever
   the trouble is, something is missing — contradicts §7, where the sneeze is
   caused by qi being harmonious and abundant. The chapter does not notice. This
   is the episode's honest turn: the system is complete, elegant, internally
   consistent almost everywhere, and it has a seam.

6. **The model has exactly one body in it.** Every answer above describes qi
   moving inside a single person. **Contagious yawning — which is real,
   documented across humans, chimpanzees and dogs, and still unexplained — is a
   two-body phenomenon, and this framework has no way to represent it.** The
   Emperor sent everyone out of the room before he asked. Nothing in the chapter
   could have been discovered any other way, and the one thing it could never have
   found is the thing that happens when there are two of you.

**One thing to keep straight for any on-screen tally.** Twelve riddles, twelve
answers, twelve items in §15's recap — but **§14's list of deficiency signs (the
swimming eyes, the tipping head, the rumbling intestines, the withering limbs) is
not part of the twelve.** It is a separate list, describing what the *absence*
looks like at three levels of the body. Counting those in gives a wrong number
that a viewer following along will catch.
