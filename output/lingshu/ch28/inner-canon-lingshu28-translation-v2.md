# Lingshu, Chapter 28 — 口問篇第二十八 (v2)

**English translation for the series, second rendering.** *Kǒu Wèn* — "Oral
Inquiry": twelve things a body does that nobody ordered it to do.

> A dramatized adaptation of a classical philosophical text. Not medical advice.
> Nothing below is a diagnosis, a remedy or a protocol, and nothing below should
> be read as one. The chapter closes each answer with a needling prescription;
> those clauses are translated here for completeness, marked as such, and are
> **excluded from the episode entirely**.

## Why there is a second translation

**v1 is not superseded and is not wrong.** `inner-canon-lingshu28-translation-v1.md`
renders this chapter plainly, for a cut whose argument was about *the frame* — an
emperor clearing a room to ask what is not in the classics. This rendering serves
a different cut, and it is made on a different principle.

**v2's governing principle: track the agent.** The v2 script's whole argument is
about **who or what is the subject of each answer** — whether anything in the
sentence can be said to have decided, ordered, or chosen. So every clause below is
rendered to keep the Chinese subject where the Chinese put it, even where English
would prefer a smoother construction:

- 陽引而上，陰引而下 becomes *"the yang draws upward, the yin draws downward"* —
  **not** *"one is pulled up and down"*, which hides that yang and yin are the ones
  doing it and the person is not.
- 故數欠 becomes *"and therefore one yawns, and yawns again"* — the impersonal
  「one」 is kept, because the Chinese has no agent there and a smoother *"you
  yawn"* would invent one.
- 心動則五藏六府皆搖 becomes *"the heart being moved, all five zang and six fu are
  shaken"* — the participial chain is kept, because §9 is the **one place** in the
  chapter where a cause is traced back to a person, and blurring the links loses
  the contrast the episode is built on.

Where v1 chose readability, v2 chooses grammatical fidelity. **Neither is more
correct; they answer different questions.** Use v1's file with the v1 cut and this
one with the v2 cut, and do not mix them inside a single document.

**What is unchanged, and said plainly:** the 原文 below is the same transcription
as v1 — same source, same characters, same conversion. The Chinese did not change
because a second script was written, and presenting it as freshly re-derived would
be a fiction. **The English and every note are new.**

## About this translation

The source is the received classical text of this chapter, compiled around the
Han period and long in the **public domain**. **This English rendering is
original work made for this series**, not a reproduction of any published
translation.

**Provenance.** The user supplied the chapter as a **PDF** (a ctext-style
bilingual page, captured 2026-07-08, 5 pp.). `ctext.org`, `zh.wikisource.org` and
`8bei8.com` are all **unreachable from the repo host** — the agent proxy denies
them at CONNECT — so a hand-supplied PDF is the standing route into this repo.
Extracting it required decoding the PDF's own ToUnicode CMaps: `pdftotext` is not
installed here and `pip install` is not permitted.

The PDF also carries a facing English translation. **It was read and set aside**;
where the two differ, the difference is recorded in *Four places the supplied
English goes wrong* rather than silently absorbed.

Simplified in the source, **traditional** below, matching the rest of `output/`.
Two orthographic notes: the source writes **歧伯** where most editions print
**岐伯** (the English uses *Qi Bo* throughout), and **寫** where later editions
print **瀉**, *to drain* — the older orthography for the same word, not a
different instruction.

### ⚠ The chapter number needs qualifying on screen

This is **Lingshu 28** — the twenty-eighth chapter of the 靈樞, the *Spiritual
Pivot*. It is **not Su Wen 28** (通評虛實論), a different chapter entirely. Both
halves of the canon run to eighty-one chapters and are numbered separately, so an
unqualified "Chapter 28" is ambiguous by construction. **Cite it on screen as
*Lingshu 28*, or as *Spiritual Pivot, chapter 28*.** The folder and file names
carry the book for the same reason — `output/lingshu/ch28/`,
`inner-canon-lingshu28-*.md`, per `CLAUDE.md` § Structure. The input script was
`Chapt_LS28__why_we_yawn.pdf`.

### Conventions

Each riddle is named by a single Chinese character English has no single word
for. The character is kept, transliterated, and glossed on first use: 欠 *qiàn*,
噦 *yuě*, 唏 *xī*, 噫 *ài*, 嚏 *tì*, 嚲 *duǒ*. Technical terms stay transliterated
where English has no honest equivalent (*qi*, *yin/yang*, *zang/fu*, *shaoyin*,
*taiyang*). Bracketed words are supplied for sense and are not in the Chinese.

**Each riddle below carries an `AGENT:` line.** That is apparatus for the v2
script, not part of the translation — it names the grammatical subject of the
answer, so the script can be checked against the text rather than against memory.

**Needling prescriptions** are set off as `**[Needling — not used in the
episode]**`. They are translated because a translation that silently drops a
clause is not a translation. They appear nowhere else.

## 1. The room is cleared

> 黃帝閒居，辟左右而問於歧伯曰：余已聞九針之經，論陰陽逆順，六經已畢，願得口問。

*The Yellow Emperor, at leisure, dismissed those to left and right, and asked Qi
Bo: "I have heard the canon of the nine needles; I have discussed yin and yang,
contrary and compliant; I have finished with the six channels. Now I wish to ask
**by mouth**."*

> 歧伯避席再拜曰：善乎哉問也，此先師之所口傳也。

*Qi Bo withdrew from his mat, bowed twice, and said: "A fine question. This is
what my former teachers handed on **by mouth**."*

> 黃帝曰：願聞口傳。

*The Yellow Emperor said: "I wish to hear what was handed on by mouth."*

> 歧伯答曰：夫百病之始生也，皆生於風雨寒暑，陰陽喜怒，飲食居處，大驚卒恐。

*Qi Bo replied: "The hundred diseases, at their arising, all arise **from** wind
and rain, cold and heat; **from** yin and yang, joy and anger; **from** food and
drink and dwelling; **from** great alarm and sudden terror."*

> 則血氣分離，陰陽破敗，經絡厥絕，脈道不通，陰陽相逆，衛氣稽留，經脈虛空，血氣不次，乃失其常。

*"Then blood and qi part company; yin and yang are broken; the channels and
collaterals reverse and are cut; the roads of the vessels do not open; yin and
yang run counter to one another; defensive qi is detained in transit; the channel
vessels stand empty; blood and qi fall out of sequence — and the constancy is
lost."*

> 論不在經者，請道其方。

*"As for what is not in the classics — allow me to set out its method."*

**AGENT:** none. Every cause in the opening list is a **circumstance** — weather,
emotion, diet, dwelling, fright. Note what is absent from a Han-dynasty account of
where illness comes from: no god, no ancestor, no curse, no moral fault. The
causes are all things that happen *to* a person, and the verbs that follow (part,
break, reverse, empty) have **the body's own components as their subjects**.

**Note.** Two moves the rest of the chapter depends on. 辟左右 — *he dismissed
those to left and right* — arranges for the questions to be unwitnessed. 論不在
經者 — *what is not in the classics* — declares the material to lie outside the
written canon, handed on 口傳, teacher to student, by mouth. It was then written
down, which is the chapter's standing irony.

## 2. 欠 — the yawn

> 黃帝曰：人之欠者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **yawns** — what qi makes it so?"*

Note 使然, *makes it so*: the question already assumes the answer is a **what**
and not a **who**.

> 歧伯答曰：衛氣晝日行於陽，夜半則行於陰，陰者主夜，夜者臥；陽者主上，陰者主下；

*Qi Bo replied: "Defensive qi travels in the yang through the daylight; at
midnight it travels in the yin. Yin governs the night, and night is for lying
down; yang governs what is above, yin governs what is below."*

> 故陰氣積於下，陽氣未盡，陽引而上，陰引而下，陰陽相引，故數欠。

*"So yin qi accumulates below while yang qi is not yet spent. **The yang draws
upward. The yin draws downward. Yin and yang draw against one another** — and
therefore one yawns, and yawns again."*

> 陽氣盡，陰氣盛，則目瞑；陰氣盡而陽氣盛，則寤矣。

*"Yang qi spent and yin qi full — the eyes close. Yin qi spent and yang qi full —
one wakes."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 寫足少陰，補足太陽。
> *Drain foot shaoyin; supplement foot taiyang.*

**AGENT:** **qi, and the hour.** The verbs 行 *travels*, 積 *accumulates*, 引
*draws* all take **qi** as subject. The person appears only in 數欠, *one yawns*
— and there as the thing it happens to. 主 in 陰者主夜 is the weakest sense of
*govern*: winter governs cold, evening governs dark. A regularity, named.

**Note.** The chapter's crown jewel. The answer is a **tug of war** — two forces
pulling opposite ways at one moment, the yawn as the tremor in the rope. And it
does not stop: the same sentence, continued, produces falling asleep and waking.
**One mechanism, three phenomena.** That is what distinguishes a theory from a
list.

It is also wrong as physiology. What it gets right is *when*: yawning does cluster
at the hinge between sleep and waking, the most robust descriptive finding about
yawning in the modern literature. Right about the timing, wrong about the cause —
a distinction that recurs and must never be collapsed.

## 3. 噦 — the hiccup

> 黃帝曰：人之噦者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **hiccups** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：穀入於胃，胃氣上注於肺。今有故寒氣與新穀氣，俱還入於胃，新故相亂，真邪相攻，氣並相逆，復出於胃，故為噦。

*Qi Bo said: "Grain enters the stomach; stomach qi pours upward into the lung.
Now if old cold qi and new grain qi return together into the stomach, **new and
old fall into confusion; the true and the aberrant attack one another; the qi
combine and run counter**; it issues from the stomach again — and so there is a
hiccup."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補手太陰，寫足少陰。
> *Supplement hand taiyin; drain foot shaoyin.*

**AGENT:** **grain and cold.** Four consecutive clauses whose subjects are
substances: 穀 *grain*, 胃氣 *stomach qi*, 寒氣 *cold qi*, 氣 *qi*. The only verb
of conflict — 攻, *attack* — has qi on both sides of it. Nothing chooses.

**Note.** 噦 is the hiccup, the involuntary catch with the sound made on the
intake. The supplied English renders it *"sighs"*, which is wrong and collides
with §10, where 太息 is a separate riddle with a separate answer.

The grammar here is shared by eight of the twelve: **something arrives, something
reverses, it leaves the way it came in.**

## 4. 唏 — the catching sob

> 黃帝曰：人之唏者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sobs — the shuddering catch of breath
after weeping** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：此陰氣盛而陽氣虛，陰氣疾而陽氣徐，陰氣盛而陽氣絕，故為唏。

*Qi Bo said: "This is yin qi full and yang qi depleted; yin qi quick and yang qi
slow; yin qi flooding until yang qi is cut off — and so there is the catching
sob."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陽，寫足少陰。
> *Supplement foot taiyang; drain foot shaoyin.*

**AGENT:** **two quantities and their rates.** Note that this riddle follows
weeping in ordinary experience but is answered **without any reference to grief**
— the sob is given a purely mechanical account while §9 gives the tears an
emotional one. The chapter does not connect them.

**Note — a genuine dispute, and we take a side.** 唏 has two readings. The
mainstream one, taken here, reads it as 歔欷, the involuntary shuddering intake of
breath that follows crying. The minority reading, which the supplied English
follows, makes it *shivering and teeth-chattering*.

Rejected on internal grounds: **§5 is 振寒, which already is shivering**, with its
own separate answer. Reading §4 the same way gives two consecutive riddles about
one act with two different explanations, and leaves the canon's most conspicuous
emotional reflex absent from a chapter that catalogues every other involuntary
noise a body makes. The episode states the dispute on screen rather than hiding
it.

## 5. 振寒 — the shiver

> 黃帝曰：人之振寒者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **shivers with cold** — what qi makes it
so?"*

> 歧伯曰：寒氣客於皮膚，陰氣盛，陽氣虛，故為振寒寒慄，補諸陽。

*Qi Bo said: "Cold qi **lodges as a guest** in the skin; yin qi is full, yang qi
depleted — and so there is shivering and trembling with cold."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補諸陽。
> *Supplement all the yang [channels].*

**AGENT:** **the cold.** 客 — *lodges as a guest* — is the standard verb for a
pathogen taking up residence, and it is the most agentive verb in the chapter: the
cold is the one doing something, and the body is the premises. The person is not
in the sentence at all.

**Note.** The shortest answer: one line, no cascade, no staging.

## 6. 噫 — the belch

> 黃帝曰：人之噫者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **belches** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：寒氣客於胃，厥逆從下上散，復出於胃，故為噫。

*Qi Bo said: "Cold qi lodges as a guest in the stomach; turning back, it
disperses upward from below; it issues from the stomach again — and so there is a
belch."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陰陽明，一曰補眉本也。
> *Supplement foot taiyin and yangming. **Another says**: supplement at the root
> of the eyebrow.*

**AGENT:** **the cold, again.** Same guest, different room.

**Note — 一曰, "another says."** The most interesting three characters in the
chapter. The text records a **second, incompatible opinion and does not
adjudicate**. It happens again in §7 (*another says: above the eyebrow*) and in
§15 (*another says: below the outer ankle*).

That is what an oral tradition looks like once it is written down: two teachers
said two things, the student had heard both, and he kept both. A chapter that
opened by announcing it was **not in the classics** preserves its own
disagreements — which is the strongest internal evidence that it was transmitted
the way it says it was.

## 7. 嚏 — the sneeze

> 黃帝曰：人之嚏者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sneezes** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：陽氣和利，滿於心，出於鼻，故為嚏。

*Qi Bo said: "**Yang qi is harmonious and running freely**; it fills the heart;
it issues from the nose — and so there is a sneeze."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足太陽榮眉本，一曰眉上也。
> *Supplement the foot taiyang [channel] at the root of the eyebrow. Another
> says: above the eyebrow.*

**AGENT:** **yang qi, and it is not in difficulty.** The only riddle whose subject
is doing well rather than badly. 和利 — *harmonious and running freely* — is a
phrase of health, not of disorder.

**Note.** The odd one out, and §14 will run straight over it. Every other answer
is a story about deficiency, cold, or disorder; §14 then declares that **all
twelve** are cases of something missing. The sneeze is standing right there,
explained by abundance. **The unifying claim does not fit its own list, and the
chapter does not notice.**

## 8. 嚲 — limpness

> 黃帝曰：人之嚲者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **body goes slack — the limb hanging
heavy that will not answer** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：胃不實則諸脈虛；諸脈虛則筋脈懈惰；筋脈懈惰則行陰用力，氣不能復，故為嚲。

*Qi Bo said: "The stomach not full, then all the vessels are empty. All the
vessels empty, then the sinews and vessels go slack and idle. The sinews and
vessels slack and idle, then **[when] one exerts oneself in the yin**, the qi
cannot be restored — and so there is limpness."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 因其所在，補分肉間。
> *According to where it is, supplement between the flesh divisions.*

**AGENT:** **a chain of conditions, and no one at the end of it.** Four links,
each stated as 則 — *then*. The most machine-like answer in the chapter, and the
closest it comes to an algorithm.

**Note — two corrections.** **嚲 is not drooling.** It means *drooping, hanging
slack*; here the limbs going heavy and unresponsive. The supplied English renders
it *"drools"*, which collides with §11, where 涎下 is a separate riddle. This is
the second of two collisions that would reduce the twelve to ten.

Second, **行陰用力** is ambiguous: *exerting oneself in the yin [parts, i.e.
moving the lower body]*, or *sexual exertion*. Commentators take both. The neutral
reading is used here and in the episode, on the principle applied to 作強 in this
repo's Su Wen 8 translation (since removed from the repo) — where two readings are
defensible, take the one the words best support and do not stage the other.

## 9. 泣涕 — weeping

> 黃帝曰：人之哀而泣涕出者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **grieves, and tears and nasal discharge
come out** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：心者，五藏六府之主也；目者，宗脈之所聚也，上液之道也；口鼻者，氣之門戶也。

*Qi Bo said: "**The heart is the ruler of the five zang and the six fu.** The eyes
are where the great converging vessels gather, and the road by which fluid
ascends. The mouth and nose are the gates through which qi passes in and out."*

> 故悲哀愁憂則心動，心動則五藏六府皆搖，搖則宗脈感，宗脈感則液道開，液道開，故泣涕出焉。

*"So: sorrow, grief, care and worry — **then the heart is moved**. The heart
moved, then all five zang and six fu are **shaken**. Shaken, then the great
converging vessels **respond**. The vessels responding, then the fluid road
**opens**. The road open — and so the tears and the discharge come out."*

> 液者，所以灌精濡空竅者也，故上液之道開則泣，泣不止則液竭；

*"Fluid is that which irrigates the essence and moistens the hollow orifices. So
the upward road of fluid open, then one weeps; the weeping not ceasing, then the
fluid is exhausted."*

> 液竭則精不灌，精不灌則目無所見矣，故命曰奪精。

*"The fluid exhausted, then the essence is not irrigated; the essence not
irrigated, then **the eyes see nothing**. And so it is named **奪精 — the robbing
of the essence**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補天柱經俠頸。
> *Supplement the Tianzhu channel, flanking the neck.*

**AGENT: 主 — a ruler. This is the hinge of the whole chapter, and of the v2
script.** For eight riddles the subject of every answer has been a substance, a
quantity or an hour. Here, in one clause, the chapter installs 心者，五藏六府之主也
— *the heart is the ruler of the five zang and the six fu* — and the causal chain
begins not in the weather but in **悲哀愁憂**, sorrow and grief and care and
worry. The first link is a state of mind, and a state of mind belongs to somebody.

**Note.** The longest and best-built answer in the chapter: a **chain of custody
for a single tear**, grief → the heart moves → every organ shakes → the vessels
answer → a road opens → water leaves the face. Every link named, in order, running
from an emotion to a fluid without once leaving the body.

**The closing claim is false and the episode says so immediately.** Weeping does
not exhaust a finite reservoir, and crying does not take your eyesight. 奪精 is a
beautiful phrase fastened to a wrong mechanism, and the series' rule is to say
which is which rather than let the phrase carry the claim.

## 10. 太息 — the long sigh

> 黃帝曰：人之太息者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **sighs — the long breath drawn and let
go** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：憂思則心系急，心系急則氣道約，約則不利，故太息以伸出之，

*Qi Bo said: "**Worry and brooding, then the cords of the heart draw tight.** The
cords of the heart tight, then the road of qi is constricted. Constricted, then it
does not run freely — and so one sighs, **to stretch it open and let it out**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補手少陰心主、足少陽留之也。
> *Supplement hand shaoyin and the heart governor, and foot shaoyang; retain
> [the needle].*

**AGENT: 憂思 — worry and brooding.** The second and last riddle whose chain
begins in a mind rather than in the weather. With §9 it forms the only pair in the
chapter where something a person is doing causes something the person's body then
does.

**Note.** The chapter's best score against the modern account. Two claims: that
sighing keeps company with worry, and that its purpose is to **open something
constricted**. The first is correct as an association — sighing does increase with
anxiety. The second is closer to right than anything else here: the physiological
job of a spontaneous sigh is to reinflate collapsed portions of lung and restore
compliance, and a dedicated brainstem circuit generates them. **Constriction, and
a breath that opens it, is the right shape.** The organ is wrong — the lung, not
the cords of the heart — and the episode says so rather than rounding a near-miss
up to a hit.

## 11. 涎下 — drooling

> 黃帝曰：人之涎下者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **saliva runs down** — what qi makes it
so?"*

> 歧伯曰：飲食者，皆入於胃，胃中有熱則蟲動，蟲動則胃緩，胃緩則廉泉開，故涎下，

*Qi Bo said: "Food and drink all enter the stomach. **Heat in the stomach, then
the worms stir. The worms stirring, then the stomach slackens. The stomach slack,
then Lianquan opens** — and so the saliva runs down."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足少陰。
> *Supplement foot shaoyin.*

**AGENT:** **the worms** — the only living thing with its own motion anywhere in
the twelve, and it is a parasite rather than the person. 蟲 is meant literally.
Intestinal parasites were an ordinary fact of life and the text reaches for them
the way it reaches for cold and wind.

**Note.** 廉泉 *Lianquan* — "the modest spring" — names the point beneath the
tongue where saliva was held to arise, conventionally identified with the point
later standardised as CV23. The chapter treats it as a valve. The episode keeps the
worms in: a mechanism that is specific, physical, checkable **and wrong** teaches
more than a vague one that could never be wrong at all.

## 12. 耳鳴 — ringing in the ears

> 黃帝曰：人之耳中鳴者，何氣使然？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person's **ears ring** — what qi makes it so?"*

> 歧伯曰：耳者，宗脈之所聚也，故胃中空則宗脈虛，虛則下溜，脈有所竭者，故耳鳴，

*Qi Bo said: "The ears are where the great converging vessels gather. So the
stomach empty, then the converging vessels are depleted; depleted, then they drain
downward; and where a vessel has run dry — the ear rings."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補客主人，手大指爪甲上與肉交者也。
> *Supplement Kezhuren, and [the point] on the thumb where the nail meets the
> flesh.*

**AGENT:** **an empty stomach.** Note the structural economy and the cost of it:
**the ears and the eyes are given the same premise** — both are where the 宗脈
converge. That single claim explains tears in §9 and tinnitus here. It is the
chapter's system working exactly as designed, and it is wrong in both places.

**Note — the supplied English merges two points into one.** It reads *"Kezhuoren
(TE21), which is located at the junction of the flesh and nail bed on the thumb"*
— but 客主人 *Kezhuren* is a point in front of the ear (the classical name for the
point later standardised as GB3), and 手大指爪甲上與肉交者 is a **separate** point
at the corner of the thumbnail (later standardised as LU11). One is beside the ear
and one is on the hand. Recorded for accuracy; moot for the episode, which uses no
needling material.

## 13. 自齧舌 — biting your own tongue

> 黃帝曰：人之自齧舌者，何氣使然？此厥逆走上，脈氣輩至也。

*The Yellow Emperor said: "When a person **bites his own tongue** — what qi makes
it so?" [Qi Bo said:] "This is qi turning back and running upward, the vessel-qi
**arriving out of turn**."*

> 少陰氣至則齧舌，少陽氣至則齧頰，陽明氣至則齧唇矣。

*"Shaoyin qi arriving, then one bites the **tongue**. Shaoyang qi arriving, then
the **cheek**. Yangming qi arriving, then the **lip**."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 視主病者，則補之。
> *See which [channel] governs the trouble, and supplement that one.*

**AGENT:** **qi, arriving at the wrong time.** The strangest riddle and the
best-shaped wrong answer. It is wrong — biting your tongue is a motor-timing error
between jaw and tongue. But the *form* of the answer is 輩至, **arriving out of
turn**: the explanation for an involuntary self-injury is a **scheduling
failure**.

**Note.** Being the right shape of answer is not being right, and the episode does
not round it up. The three-way mapping — tongue, cheek, lip, each to its own
channel — is a specific prediction that does not hold.

## 14. 凡此十二邪 — all these twelve

> 凡此十二邪者，皆奇邪之走空竅者也。故邪之所在，皆為不足。

*"**All these twelve** are strange qi running into the hollow openings. And so,
wherever the aberrant qi is — **in every case, something is missing**."*

> 故上氣不足，腦為之不滿，耳為之苦鳴，頭為之苦傾，目為之眩。

*"So the qi above insufficient, the brain is not filled by it, the ears suffer
ringing by it, the head suffers a tendency to tip by it, the eyes swim."*

> 中氣不足，溲便為之變，腸為之苦鳴。

*"The qi of the centre insufficient, the passing of water and stool is altered by
it, the intestines suffer rumbling by it."*

> 下氣不足，則乃為痿厥心悗。

*"The qi below insufficient, then there is withering and cold reversal, and
oppression at the heart."*

> **[Needling — not used in the episode]** 補足外踝下留之。
> *Supplement below the outer ankle and retain [the needle].*

**AGENT:** **an absence.** 皆為不足 — *in every case, something is missing* — is
the chapter's one attempt at a universal, and its subject is a **deficit**: not an
excess, not an invader with purposes of its own. Something that should be present
is not.

**Note.** The moment the anthology stops being an anthology. It is also the moment
to notice what it costs: **§7 explained the sneeze by qi being harmonious and
abundant**, and §14 sweeps it in anyway. The unifying claim does not fit its own
list.

**And the count is exact.** 十二 is not a round number. Counting the riddles as
asked: 欠 yawn, 噦 hiccup, 唏 sob, 振寒 shiver, 噫 belch, 嚏 sneeze, 嚲 limpness,
泣涕 weeping, 太息 sigh, 涎下 drooling, 耳鳴 tinnitus, 自齧舌 tongue-biting.
Twelve asked, twelve answered, twelve recapitulated in §15 — **provided §3 and §8
are translated correctly**, which is what the two collisions in the supplied
English would destroy.

**§14's own deficiency signs are not among the twelve.** The swimming eyes, the
tipping head, the rumbling intestines, the withering limbs are a *separate* list,
describing what absence looks like at three levels. Counting them in gives a
number a viewer following along will catch.

## 15. How they are treated

> 黃帝曰：治之奈何？

*The Yellow Emperor said: "How are these treated?"*

> **[Needling — the whole of this section is needling prescription and none of it
> is used in the episode. Translated for completeness only.]**

> 歧伯曰：腎主為欠，取足少陰；肺主為噦，取手太陰、足少陰；唏者，陰與陽絕，故補足太陽，寫足少陰；振寒者，補諸陽；噫者，補足太陰陽明；嚏者，補足太陽眉本；嚲，因其所在，補分肉間；泣出補天柱經俠頸，俠頸者，頭中分也；太息，補手少陰、心主、足少陽，留之；涎下補足少陰；耳鳴補客主人，手大指爪甲上與肉交者；自齧舌，視主病者，則補之。

*Qi Bo said: "The kidney governs yawning — take foot shaoyin. The lung governs
hiccup — take hand taiyin and foot shaoyin. For the catching sob, yin and yang are
cut off from one another, so supplement foot taiyang and drain foot shaoyin. For
shivering with cold, supplement all the yang. For belching, supplement foot taiyin
and yangming. For sneezing, supplement foot taiyang at the root of the eyebrow.
For limpness, according to where it is, supplement between the flesh divisions.
For weeping, supplement the Tianzhu channel flanking the neck — 'flanking the
neck' means the midline of the head. For the long sigh, supplement hand shaoyin,
the heart governor, and foot shaoyang, and retain. For saliva running down,
supplement foot shaoyin. For ringing in the ears, supplement Kezhuren, and [the
point] on the thumb where the nail meets the flesh. For biting one's own tongue,
see which [channel] governs the trouble and supplement that one."*

> 目眩頭傾，補足外踝下留之；痿厥心悗，刺足大趾間上二寸，留之，一曰足外踝下留之。

*"For swimming eyes and a head that tips, supplement below the outer ankle and
retain. For withering, cold reversal and oppression at the heart, needle two cun
above the cleft between the great toes and retain — **another says**: below the
outer ankle, and retain."*

**AGENT — and this one matters.** §15 opens with **腎主為欠**, *the kidney governs
yawning*, and **肺主為噦**, *the lung governs hiccup*. **Neither attribution
appears in §2 or §3.** The recapitulation quietly supplies organ-rulers for two
riddles that were answered without any ruler at all — which is the chapter
reconciling itself, after the fact, with the organ system used elsewhere in the
canon. It is the seam of a compiled text, visible in the last section.

And it closes on **一曰** one final time. Even the chapter's last line records a
second opinion.

## Four places the supplied English goes wrong

Three of the four change **which bodily act is being discussed**, and any of the
three breaks the count of twelve on screen.

| # | Chinese | Supplied English | This translation | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | §3 噦 | "sighs" | **hiccup** | Collides with §10 太息, the actual sigh. Two riddles become one. |
| B | §8 嚲 | "drools" | **limpness / slackness** | Collides with §11 涎下, the actual drooling. Two riddles become one. |
| C | §12 客主人 + 手大指爪甲上 | one point, "Kezhuoren (TE21) … on the thumb" | **two separate points** — one before the ear, one at the thumbnail | Merges two locations into an impossible one. Moot for the episode. |
| D | §4 唏 | "shivers or chatters their teeth" | **the catching sob** | Defensible minority reading, but it duplicates §5 振寒. The episode states the dispute rather than concealing it. |

A and B together would reduce the chapter's twelve riddles to ten while §14 still
says 十二. **Anything on screen that counts must be built from this file, not from
the supplied English.**

## Reading notes for production

Six things the v2 cut treats as load-bearing, all recoverable from the Chinese
above:

1. **The question is already agentless.** 何氣使然 — *what qi makes it so* — asks
   for a **what**, not a **who**, twelve times. The Emperor never once asks *who
   is doing this*, and the answers oblige.

2. **One mechanism, three phenomena.** §2 explains yawning, falling asleep and
   waking from a single sentence about defensive qi. That is the difference
   between a theory and a list, and it is the strongest reason to take the chapter
   seriously.

3. **Right about when, wrong about why.** Yawning does cluster at the sleep–wake
   hinge (§2); sighing does keep company with worry (§10); a full stomach really
   is a hiccup trigger (§3). Good observations, wrong mechanisms. **Separate
   scores, never merged.**

4. **§9 installs a ruler, and nothing before it needed one.** 心者，五藏六府之主也
   arrives after eight agentless answers. §15 then retro-fits two more rulers
   (腎主為欠, 肺主為噦) onto riddles that had none. The chapter is being pulled
   toward a governed body by the canon around it.

5. **The other canon chapter is the comparison.** Su Wen 8 《靈蘭祕典論》 builds the
   entire body as a government of twelve offices where nothing happens without an
   officer. **The same canon says a body is an administration and describes twelve
   daily events no administration touches.** Cite it on screen as **Su Wen 8**,
   never "chapter 6" — Su Wen 6 in the received recension is a different chapter
   (陰陽離合論). This repo produced that cut and then **deleted it in the layout
   migration**, so it is no longer available to check against and survives only in
   git history; verify any claim about it against the classical text.

6. **The chapter preserves its own disagreements.** 一曰 — *another says* —
   appears in §6, §7 and §15, unadjudicated each time.

**And the thing outside the text.** Every answer above describes qi inside **one
body**. Contagious yawning — documented in humans, chimpanzees and dogs, and still
unexplained — is a **two-body** phenomenon, and this framework has no way to
represent it. That is not a criticism the chapter could have anticipated; it is a
limit of the apparatus, and the v2 cut lands it as one.
