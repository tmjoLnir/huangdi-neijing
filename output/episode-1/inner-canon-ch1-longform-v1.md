# The Emperor's Inner Canon — Chapter 1 Longform (18 minutes)

**上古天真論篇第一 · The Body Keeps a Calendar**

**Final video (1280×720, 16:9 landscape, 18:00, MP4):** *not produced — this is a

The first longform episode in the series, and the first cut of any kind to carry
the whole of a chapter rather than a single idea from it. Source: Suwen Chapter 1,
上古天真論 — *Discourse on the Natural Purity of High Antiquity*. Chapter 1 is
already covered by two trailers (`-trailer-v1`, `-trailer-v2`), both built on the
chapter's opening sentence; this episode covers the chapter end to end, and
`inner-canon-ch1-trailer-v3.md` is its companion teaser.

> A dramatized adaptation of a classical philosophical text. Not medical advice.

## Source and translation

The classical text is Suwen Chapter 1, compiled around the Han period and long in
the public domain. **All English renderings in this document are original
translations made for this script**, working from the received classical text.
They are deliberately plain rather than literary, because the episode's argument
depends on what the text actually says — several of the beats turn on a hedge or a
conditional that smoother translations drop.

Where a rendering is contestable, the document says so and gives the Chinese, so a
reviewer can check the claim rather than take it on trust.

# COLD OPEN — blocks 1–6 · 0:00–1:00

**SOUND:** A room tone. Something wooden settling. No music yet.

**VISUAL:** Extreme close on bamboo slats, bound with cord, rolling open under a
lamp. Ink strokes surface out of the grain. No faces.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> Chinese medicine has a founding text. It is about two thousand years old, and
> nearly every idea that follows in it follows from the first page.
>
> That page is not a treatment. It is not a diagnosis. It is a complaint.

**VISUAL:** FAN-DI at a low lacquer table, gold robe, round glasses. The white fan
is **snapped open** — he is performing. He reads aloud, delighted with himself.

> **FAN-DI**
> "I have heard that the people of high antiquity all passed a hundred years, and
> their movements did not weaken."

**VISUAL:** He turns the slat over. The delight goes out of his face.

> **FAN-DI**
> "People now are worn out at half a hundred."

**SOUND:** The room drops out. Silence.

**VISUAL:** Two ink marks on paper, far apart. One long, one short.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> A hundred years. Fifty. The emperor wants to know what happened in the gap.

# ACT I — THE QUESTION · blocks 9–26 · 1:20–4:20

**VISUAL:** The court. Lamplight, low tables, scrolls. DR-QI stands with an open
bamboo scroll held casually, like a tablet. She is entirely still.

> **FAN-DI**
> So which is it? Is the world different now — or are we?

**NARRATOR (V.O.) — over a brush writing the two characters:**
> That is the emperor's real question, and it is worth noticing how modern it is.
> He is asking whether a change he can measure is a change in the environment, or
> a change in behaviour. Two thousand years later that is still the shape of every
> argument about public health.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 時世異耶？人將失之耶？
> *"Is it that the times are different — or that people have lost something?"*

**VISUAL:** DR-QI does not answer immediately. She sets the scroll down first.

> **DR-QI**
> Neither, entirely. You have quoted the line without its first two words.

> **FAN-DI**
> I quoted it exactly.

> **DR-QI**
> You did. That is the problem.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The sentence opens with 余聞 — *"I have heard."* The founding text of Chinese
> medicine begins by flagging its own opening claim as hearsay, in the same breath
> as making it. The emperor is not reporting a fact. He is repeating a rumour, and
> saying so.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 余聞上古之人，春秋皆度百歲，而動作不衰
> *"**I have heard** that the people of high antiquity all passed a hundred years,
> and their movements did not weaken."*

**VISUAL:** LEI-GONG, cinnabar red, mid-hop, spark motes, grinning.

> **LEI-GONG**
> So the book opens by admitting it doesn't know?

> **DR-QI**
> The book opens by admitting where it got it. Those are different, and the second
> one is rarer.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> And the reply is narrower still. When the physician answers, he does not say *the
> ancients* lived a hundred years. He says **those among them who knew the Way**.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 上古之人，**其知道者**
> *"Among the people of high antiquity, **those who knew the Way** —"*

**VISUAL:** A wide brush stroke, then a much smaller one inside it.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> Not everyone. A subset. The most-quoted claim in the history of Chinese medicine
> is, in its own source, conditional — and almost every later retelling drops the
> condition.

> **FAN-DI**
> *(fan set down, quiet)*
> Then what did the subset do?

# ACT II — THE FIVE HABITS · blocks 27–50 · 4:20–8:20

**SOUND:** Guqin enters, low.

**VISUAL:** Five brush strokes laid down one at a time, each becoming a small
object: a sun-and-shadow gnomon; a counting rod; a covered rice bowl; a lamp being
lit and put out; a hoe set down against a wall.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The answer is five habits, and it is worth sitting with how unremarkable they
> are.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 法於陰陽，和於術數，食飲有節，起居有常，不妄作勞
> *"They patterned themselves on yin and yang, kept in accord with the arts of
> number, ate and drank with measure, rose and rested at regular hours, and did
> not labour recklessly."*

> **LEI-GONG**
> That's it? That's the secret of the ancients? Eat properly and go to bed at a
> sensible hour?

> **DR-QI**
> Yes.

> **LEI-GONG**
> *(deflating)*
> ...Oh.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> It is an anticlimax by design. The text is not withholding a technique. Its claim
> is that there was never a technique — that the thing separating a long life from
> a short one is **regularity**, practised without interruption for decades.

**VISUAL:** The lamp lighting and extinguishing, lighting and extinguishing —
the same action, many times, speeding up.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> Which is a much harder prescription than a secret would be. A secret you can
> learn once.

**VISUAL:** Turn. The palette cools. A hall at night, seen from a distance —
warm blur, movement, no detail. Atmospheric, never close.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Then the chapter turns, and describes the opposite. It is the oldest passage in
> the book that reads like it was written about someone the author knew.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 以酒為漿，以妄為常
> *"They take wine for water, and take the reckless for the ordinary."*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Wine in place of water. Recklessness as the default setting. Appetite carried
> into every corner of a life until there is nothing held in reserve — and then, at
> fifty, the bill.

**VISUAL:** The distant hall dissolves into a dry riverbed, ink, no figures.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 故半百而衰也
> *"And so they are spent at half a hundred."*

> **FAN-DI**
> That is not a diagnosis. That is a moral judgement with a number attached.

> **DR-QI**
> It is. The canon is a moral document before it is a medical one. That is why it
> survived — and it is why you should read the number with more suspicion than the
> advice.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Note what the physician does *not* claim. Not that the habits cure anything. The
> claim is preventive and it is stated as a rhetorical question.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 恬惔虛無，真氣從之，精神內守，病安從來
> *"Calm, and empty of craving — the true qi follows. Essence and spirit are kept
> within. Where, then, would sickness come from?"*

> **LEI-GONG**
> Plenty of places. That's a terrible question.

> **DR-QI**
> It is a rhetorical question in a book that spends seventy-nine more chapters
> answering it literally. Hold the objection. You are early, not wrong.

# ACT III — THE BODY KEEPS A CALENDAR · blocks 51–80 · 8:20–13:20

**SOUND:** Guqin drops away. A single struck note.

**VISUAL:** A blank scroll. A brush begins ruling it into columns — a table
forming, in ink, from nothing.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Here the chapter stops arguing and starts counting. What follows is the most
> concrete passage in the whole canon, and the easiest to check.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> It says the body runs on a schedule. Women in cycles of seven years. Men in
> cycles of eight.

**VISUAL:** Two columns of arcs, seven on one side, eight on the other. Pure
brushwork. **No bodies, no figures, at any point in this act.**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> At seven, a girl's kidney qi flourishes; the milk teeth are replaced and the hair
> grows in. At fourteen — two sevens — the text says *tian gui* arrives, the vessels
> open, and she can bear children.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 二七而天癸至 … 故有子
> *"At two sevens the tian gui arrives … and so she can bear children."*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> *Tian gui* — 天癸, literally "heavenly water" — is the text's name for the
> maturing capacity to reproduce. It is described the way an astronomer describes a
> season: as something that arrives on a schedule and later withdraws.

**VISUAL:** The arcs continue. Twenty-one: the frame fills out. Twenty-eight: the
peak, marked with a single dense stroke. Thirty-five: the first stroke that thins.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Twenty-eight is the summit. At thirty-five the text says the decline begins — in
> the face first. At forty-two the hair starts to whiten. At forty-nine, seven
> sevens, the tian gui is exhausted and childbearing ends.

**VISUAL:** The male column. Sixteen, twenty-four, thirty-two — the peak. Forty:
the first thinning. Fifty-six. Sixty-four, where the column stops.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The men's column runs the same shape eight years at a time, peaks at thirty-two,
> and ends at sixty-four. Eight eights.

> **FAN-DI**
> *(fan open again, delighted)*
> It is a timetable. Someone wrote down the exact year my face gives up.

> **LEI-GONG**
> Thirty-five!

> **FAN-DI**
> Thank you, Lei-Gong.

**SOUND:** Beat of silence.

> **DR-QI**
> And now the part you will not enjoy. Some of it is right.

**VISUAL:** The ink arcs sit alongside a modern line — clean, plain, unlabelled
until the narrator names it.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Read as observation rather than arithmetic, the map holds up better than it has
> any right to. Puberty in the low-to-mid teens. Physical peak in the twenties to
> early thirties. Gradual decline from the mid-thirties. Fertility ending for women
> around the late forties. Those are broadly the right shapes, recorded by people
> with no instruments, no statistics, and no way to follow a cohort through time.

> **DR-QI**
> They had one method. They watched, for a very long time, and they wrote it down.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> And now what it got wrong, which matters more.

**VISUAL:** The neat arcs begin to blur at their edges — the same curve drawn
fifty times, slightly differently, becoming a band rather than a line.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The arithmetic is false. Bodies do not change on seven- and eight-year
> boundaries; nothing switches at forty-two that did not also switch at forty-one.
> The neatness is the tell — sevens for women and eights for men is a numerological
> commitment first and an observation second.
>
> The ceilings are false too. The text puts a hard limit at seven sevens and eight
> eights. Real variation is enormous, and the boundaries are soft.
>
> And the whole map describes a population, not a person. It cannot tell you
> anything about yourself. Read as a schedule for your own body, it is wrong twice
> over — wrong in the arithmetic, and wrong in kind.

> **FAN-DI**
> So it is a beautiful table that does not work.

> **DR-QI**
> It is a beautiful table that does something a working one would not. It made the
> body a thing you could *chart*. Before you can be right about the shape of a
> life, someone has to propose that it has one.

# ACT IV — FOUR KINDS OF PEOPLE · blocks 81–98 · 13:20–16:20

**SOUND:** Something wide and open. Wind.

**VISUAL:** Mountains in ink wash, receding. Very large, very empty.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The chapter ends somewhere stranger than it began. It describes four kinds of
> people, and the first kind cannot exist.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 上古有真人者，提挈天地，把握陰陽 … 壽敝天地，無有終時
> *"In high antiquity there were True Men, who took up heaven and earth and held
> yin and yang … their years outwore heaven and earth, and had no ending."*

> **LEI-GONG**
> No ending. None? They're still going?

> **FAN-DI**
> *(fan snapping shut)*
> Now we are simply telling stories.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> Yes. And this is the moment to be exact, because it is the passage that has been
> quoted for two thousand years to sell things.
>
> **There were no True Men.** Nobody has ever outworn heaven and earth. No practice
> in this text or any other has extended a human life without end, and no reading
> of this passage that treats it as a report is honest.

**VISUAL:** The mountains stay. Nothing dramatic happens to them.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> What is interesting is that the text appears to know. Because it does not stop
> at one kind of person. It gives four, and they descend.

**VISUAL:** Four brush marks, top to bottom, each smaller and more solid than the
one above.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The True Man — 真人 — placed in a remote past, beyond reach, with no ending.
>
> Below him the Perfected Man — 至人 — who withdraws from the world to practise.
>
> Below him the Sage — 聖人 — who stays *in* the world, lives among ordinary
> customs, keeps his temper, and does not exhaust himself.
>
> And last the Worthy Man — 賢人 — who studies the seasons and follows those who
> came before.

> **DR-QI**
> Read the ladder from the bottom. The two kinds you could actually be are the two
> at the bottom. And of the Sage the text claims only this —

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 形體不敝，精神不散，亦可以百數
> *"His body did not wear out, his spirit did not scatter, and he too could be
> counted in hundreds."*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> A hundred years. Not endlessness. A long ordinary life, for a person living an
> ordinary life carefully.
>
> And of the Worthy Man — the most achievable of the four — the text says something
> it did not have to say.

**ON-SCREEN TEXT:**
> 可使益壽而有極時
> *"He can be made to increase his years — but there is a limit."*

**SOUND:** Silence.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> *There is a limit.* The chapter that opens with men who never die closes by
> putting a ceiling on it. The immortality is scenery. The ceiling is the argument.

> **FAN-DI**
> *(quiet)*
> It undercuts itself.

> **DR-QI**
> It qualifies itself. A text that only ever said the impossible thing would not
> have been worth copying.

# ACT V — THE ANSWER · blocks 99–106 · 16:20–17:40

**VISUAL:** Back to the bamboo slats from the cold open, still open under the lamp.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> So — the emperor's question. Is the world different, or are we?

**VISUAL:** The two ink marks from the cold open. The long one and the short one.

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
> The chapter's answer is: we are. And it is almost certainly wrong about that.
> The people who wrote this down did not live to a hundred; by every estimate we
> have, they died younger than the ancestors they were praising. The golden age
> they were mourning did not happen.
>
> But look at what the answer *is*. Not a herb. Not a rite. Not a secret held by
> someone who died a thousand years before you were born. Sleep on a schedule. Eat
> with measure. Do not exhaust yourself. Keep your temper. Notice the season you
> are in.
>
> They dressed a claim about **how to live** as a claim about **how long**. The
> dressing was false. The claim underneath was the most durable idea in the book —
> and two thousand years of scribes copied the false sentence to keep it.

**VISUAL:** One scroll splitting into a dozen scrolls of visibly different ages,
fanning back into depth, the seven-and-eight arcs faint beneath them.

> **NARRATOR (V.O.)**
> The Emperor's Inner Canon. Chapter One.

### Finishing steps — run these on the downloaded MP4

Numbers below are this episode's own; do not copy them from a trailer document.

**1. Derive the narration table and build the sidecar.** Unlike a trailer, this
document has no narration table yet. Produce a 108-row table (block / speaker+beat
/ line) from the script above, keeping block numbers continuous, then:

```
node scripts/check_caption_fit.js output/episode-1/inner-canon-ch1-longform-v1.md --format 16:9
node scripts/build_subtitles.js  output/episode-1/inner-canon-ch1-longform-v1.md --format 16:9
```

**Both need `--format 16:9`.** The default is the 9:16 trailer profile, and using
it here computes a ~22-character budget against a 1280-wide frame — captions come
out roughly half the size they should be.

**2. Burn the captions.** Use the printed `ffmpeg` command. If burning onto a 480p
draft rather than a 1280×720 render, add `scale=1280:720,` as the first filter in
the chain — libass takes its resolution from the video, so a sidecar computed for
1280×720 renders wrong against a smaller frame. Anton must be installed locally
(`fc-list | grep -i anton`) or libass silently substitutes another face.

Strip the end-card cues (the last two, over blocks 107–108) on a copy before
burning, so the disclaimer does not appear as a caption *and* on the card.

**3. History lower-third** — fade in ~0:02, hold to ~0:09. Place it above the
