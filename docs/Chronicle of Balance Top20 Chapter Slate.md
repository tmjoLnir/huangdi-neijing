# Chronicle of Balance — Top 20 Chapter Slate
### Tier-1 Longform Video Selection, RPM Modelling, Compliance Assessment & Publish Sequence

**Source text:** *The Emperor's Inner Canon* (黄帝内经), ctext version — 563 pp., 162 chapters (Suwen 素问 ×81, Lingshu 灵枢经 ×81)
**Target geography:** Tier-1 (US / UK / Canada / Australia)
**Format assumption:** 18:30+ runtime, native-English VO, three-character animated cast (Fan-Di, Dr. Qi, Xiao-Lei)
**Framing:** Intellectual and cultural history — *not* health or wellness guidance
**Prepared for review by:** Joshua Chin

---

## 1. How this slate was built

I read the full chapter list and then read the actual English text of ~30 candidate chapters from the supplied document rather than selecting from reputation. Several chapters that are famous inside the tradition (Suwen 74, the Nineteen Pathomechanisms; Suwen 31, the six-day febrile progression) rank poorly here precisely *because* they are clinically load-bearing — they are diagnostic frameworks, and a diagnostic framework narrated by an animated character is the exact artifact that trips YouTube's advertiser-friendly and medical-misinformation reviews. Conversely, several chapters that are minor inside the tradition (Lingshu 28, on yawning and hiccups; Suwen 81, on tears) are outstanding here, because they are pure curiosity content with no actionable medical surface at all.

**Three filters were applied, in this order:**

1. **Compliance floor (hard filter).** Any chapter whose *central content* is a treatment protocol, a dosage, a prognosis-of-death, a needling depth instruction, or a symptom-to-remedy mapping was cut regardless of how dramatic it is. This eliminated roughly 90 of the 162 chapters outright — most of Suwen 30–65 and Lingshu 19–27.
2. **Tier-1 category value.** Chapters were sorted into the advertiser categories they will actually be classified into: *history & documentary*, *psychology & self-understanding*, *astronomy & natural science*, *ethics & professional practice*. These are the four bands that clear $6+ RPM in Tier-1. Anything that classifies as *symptom / condition / remedy* was pushed down.
3. **Narrative load-bearing capacity.** Can this chapter carry 18:30 without padding? A chapter needs at least three of: a scene, a conflict, a counter-intuitive claim, a testable proposition, a memorable image, and a turn. Chapters that are catalogues of acupoints (Suwen 58–60, Lingshu 2) have none of these.

**Already-produced chapters are excluded from the twenty.** Suwen 1–8, 11, 15, 21, 24 and Lingshu 4, 11 are in the can. Several would otherwise rank very highly (Suwen 8, the organs-as-ministers; Suwen 5, yin-yang correspondence). See §7.

## 2. The scoring framework

### 2.1 RPM model

RPM bands below are **planning estimates**, not forecasts. They assume: 100% Tier-1 audience mix, 18:30+ runtime with 2–3 mid-rolls enabled, standard ad load, and a rolling 12-month average. They are stated as ranges because the dominant variance drivers are *not* topic — they are watch time, actual geo split, and calendar seasonality.

| Advertiser category | Tier-1 RPM band | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Psychology & self-understanding | $8–16 | Highest advertiser competition; adjacency to education apps, professional services, subscription media |
| History of science / astronomy / anatomy | $7–14 | Strong documentary-adjacent demand; high male 25–54 index |
| Ethics, pedagogy, professional practice | $7–14 | Professional-services and B2B adjacency |
| General history & culture | $6–12 | Baseline documentary band |
| Longevity / ageing (even historically framed) | $5–11 | Title-level algorithmic drag; some advertisers exclude on keyword |
| Symptom / condition / remedy | $3–7 | Restricted category; frequent limited-ads flags |
| Death, prognosis, graphic pathology | $2–5 | Limited or no ads |

**Seasonality multiplier** (apply to any band): Q4 ≈ 1.4–1.8×, January ≈ 0.6–0.7×, February–March ≈ 0.8×, mid-year ≈ 1.0×. This has a larger effect on any single episode's RPM than the difference between two adjacent chapters on this list, which is why §6 schedules the top-band episodes into Q4 and the lower-band episodes into the January trough.

### 2.2 Compliance model

Four distinct exposures, which are frequently conflated:

- **A. Advertiser-Friendly Guidelines** — the yellow-icon risk. Triggered by controversial issues, harmful/dangerous acts, adult content, recreational drugs, alcohol.
- **B. Medical misinformation (Community Guidelines)** — narrowly scoped in practice (vaccines, reproductive health, harmful substances, treatment claims that contradict health-authority guidance) but *broadly enforced by automation*. The mitigation is not avoidance of medical content — it is the on-camera reckoning that separates historical claim from present-day fact.
- **C. Generic / Repetitive Content (monetisation policy)** — the structural risk. Twenty episodes of the same three characters in the same Q&A form is the failure mode. Every episode below is assigned a *distinct structural form*.
- **D. Synthetic-content disclosure** — the three-character cast is AI-generated. YouTube requires the *altered or synthetic content* disclosure where realistic content could mislead. Stylised animation of fictional characters is a lower-risk case, but the safe posture is: disclose in the description on every episode, and never let a character make a first-person factual assertion about their own expertise. This is currently the most under-weighted risk in the project.

**Risk levels used below:** Very Low / Low / Low–Moderate / Moderate.

### 1. Lingshu 28 《口问 — Oral Inquiry》
**Working title:** *Why We Yawn*

**Summary.** The Emperor dismisses his attendants and asks Qi Bo a series of questions that appear in none of the classical texts — the small, undignified questions. Why do we yawn? Why do we hiccup? Why do we sigh? Why do we sneeze, shiver, weep without sadness, get ringing in the ears, lose feeling in a limb, bite our own tongue? Qi Bo answers each in turn using a single unified model: defensive qi moving between yang and yin, day and night, upper and lower. The chapter is explicitly framed as *oral transmission* — knowledge too trivial to write down, passed teacher to student.

**Why chosen.** This is the single most clickable chapter in the entire canon and it contains almost nothing that could be construed as medical advice. Everyone yawns. Nobody knows why. The chapter offers a complete, internally consistent, and entirely wrong answer from two thousand years ago — which is the perfect shape for history-of-science content: the reader gets the pleasure of a system, and the pleasure of seeing where it fails. The framing device (the Emperor clearing the room to ask embarrassing questions) is a gift.

**Structural form.** Anthology of riddles — eleven rapid-fire questions, each a self-contained 90-second unit, escalating from trivial to strange.

**RPM:** **$9–15.** Curiosity/education classification with no sensitive-topic surface at all. High replay and high comment volume ("wait, why *do* we yawn?"). The unusually clean category read is what puts it at the top of the band.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** No treatment, no condition, no prognosis. The only care needed is not to present the qi explanation as the current scientific account — a 60-second reckoning naming the modern hypotheses (brain thermoregulation, arousal-state transition, contagious yawning as social signalling; all still unsettled) does the work and is genuinely more interesting than pretending certainty.

### 2. Suwen 13 《移精变气论 — Shifting Spirit and Transforming Vital Energy》
**Working title:** *When Medicine Stopped Being Magic*

**Summary.** The Emperor asks a dangerous question: he has heard that in ancient times illness was cured by *zhuyou* — incantation, the shifting of spirit — and that today it requires toxic drugs and needles, and still fails half the time. Why? Qibo's answer is not that the ancients had better magic. It is that they had simpler lives: living among birds and beasts, without ambition or contention, disease could not penetrate deeply, so a ritual sufficed. Now, with worry within and toil without, illness goes to the marrow, and words cannot reach it. The chapter then pivots to colour and pulse as the two instruments of real diagnosis, and closes with a lament that the physicians of the present age have lost the method.

**Why chosen.** This is the "science versus superstition" hook the project has wanted to A/B test, and it is *in the source text itself* — the canon is arguing with its own past. That is a far stronger and more defensible position than a modern narrator adjudicating from outside. It gives the series its thesis statement: this is a text about the transition from ritual to observation, caught mid-transition.

**Structural form.** Diptych — two parallel timelines crosscut throughout (the ancients / the present), converging only in the final act.

**RPM:** **$8–14.** History-of-science classification. The "how ancient people thought about X" format indexes well with the 25–54 documentary audience.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** Contains no treatment instruction. Mentions "toxic medicines" only as a historical category. The single care point: do not let the incantation material read as an endorsement of faith healing — the chapter itself explicitly says incantation no longer works, which is the correct beat to land on.

### 3. Lingshu 12 《经水 — Great Rivers》
**Working title:** *The Rivers Inside*

**Summary.** The twelve channels of the body are mapped, one to one, onto the twelve great rivers of China — differing in depth, width, and length exactly as the rivers do. Chi Bo's answer contains one of the most consequential sentences in Chinese medical literature: heaven cannot be measured and earth cannot be quantified, *but a man of eight chi in height — his skin and flesh are here; externally they can be measured and palpated, and when he is dead, one can dissect him and see them.*

**Why chosen.** The dissection line is the episode. It is the earliest explicit statement in the Chinese medical corpus that the interior of the body is an object of empirical inspection — and it sits inside a chapter that is otherwise pure analogy. That tension (we can measure this, and also it is a river system) *is* the intellectual history of the text in miniature. Visually this is the richest chapter on the slate: cartography, hydrology, and anatomy in the same frame.

**Structural form.** Expedition — a survey journey downstream, one river at a time, with the dissection line placed as the turn at the midpoint.

**RPM:** **$8–14.** Science/anatomy/history triple classification. Cartographic visuals support strong retention.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** Dissection is discussed, not depicted. Keep all imagery diagrammatic and ink-wash; no photographic or realistic anatomical rendering. The needling-depth material at the end of the chapter should be cut entirely — it adds nothing and it is the only part that could classify as instruction.

### 4. Lingshu 8 《本神 — Spirit》
**Working title:** *The Ancient Map of the Mind*

**Summary.** The Emperor asks for definitions, and gets a cascade: what comes from heaven in us is virtue; what comes from earth is qi. That which brings forth life is *jing* (essence). When two essences meet, that is *shen* (spirit). What comes and goes with the spirit is *hun*. What enters and leaves with the essence is *po*. What lets us handle the external world is *xin* (mind/heart). What the mind retains is *yi* (intention). Where intention settles is *zhi* (will). Thought that arises from will and changes is *si*. Deliberation reaching toward distant aims is *lü*. Wisdom is what results when deliberation is applied rightly.

**Why chosen.** This is a complete taxonomy of mind, built in a single paragraph, around 2,000 years old, with terms English does not have. For a Tier-1 audience it lands squarely in the highest-value category on YouTube — the intersection of psychology, philosophy of mind, and comparative intellectual history. It also sets up a genuine comparative argument (against Plato's tripartite soul, against the Aristotelian faculties, against modern cognitive taxonomies) that gives the episode intellectual weight without requiring any medical claim.

**Structural form.** Lexicon — the glossary is built on screen, term by term, each new word defined only in terms of the words already established. The visual is a diagram assembling itself.

**RPM:** **$9–16.** Highest band on the slate. Psychology-of-mind content attracts education, productivity, and professional-services advertisers, and the topic has unusually low seasonality.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** Definitional and philosophical throughout. The chapter's later sections on emotional damage to the organs should be handled as historical psychology, not as a mental-health claim — and the episode should not offer any coping advice of any kind.

### 5. Lingshu 43 《淫邪发梦 — Dreams Caused by Pathogenic Influences》
**Working title:** *The Dream Catalogue*

**Summary.** A complete system of dream interpretation on physiological rather than symbolic grounds. Excess yin: dreams of crossing great waters, and fear. Excess yang: dreams of great fires. Both in excess: dreams of mutual killing. Excess above: dreams of flying. Excess below: dreams of falling. Extreme hunger: dreams of taking. Extreme fullness: dreams of giving. Then organ by organ — liver excess brings dreams of anger; lung, of weeping and flight; heart, of laughter mixed with dread; spleen, of singing and heaviness of body.

**Why chosen.** Dream content performs, and this is a version of it that is genuinely novel to a Western audience: a dream theory that is neither Freudian nor divinatory but *somatic* — the dream as a readout of the body's internal state. The flying/falling pair alone is a hook, because it is one of the few dream universals actually documented in modern sleep research. The comparative frame (Artemidorus, Freud, activation-synthesis) writes itself.

**Structural form.** Nested frame — the episode is structured as a dream within a dream, each layer resolving one class of the catalogue.

**RPM:** **$8–14.** Psychology classification. Very high comment and share behaviour; dream content reliably generates "this happened to me" engagement.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** The risk is presentational, not topical: the catalogue must not be offered as a diagnostic tool. Never phrase it as "if you dream of falling, your X is deficient." Frame as: *this is what a physician in the Han dynasty believed a dream told him.* One explicit reckoning line handles it.

### 6. Lingshu 64 《阴阳二十五人 — The Twenty-Five Types》
**Working title:** *Twenty-Five Kinds of Person*

**Summary.** A complete personality typology: five body-and-temperament forms (wood, fire, earth, metal, water), each subdivided five ways, producing twenty-five types — each with a described physique, complexion, gait, temperament, seasonal vulnerability, and characteristic way of moving through the world. The chapter is unusual for its framing: Bo Gao says even he cannot fully understand it, the Emperor rises from his seat and performs a full ritual bow before receiving it, and swears to seal it in a golden cabinet and never publicise it.

**Why chosen.** Personality typology is one of the most durable engagement formats on the platform, and this is its deepest historical antecedent — older than the four humours in systematised form, and far more granular. The secrecy framing gives the episode a genuine dramatic spine (the Emperor bowing to his own subject) that most typology content lacks. It also permits a real intellectual payoff: why do humans keep building these systems, and why do they keep failing the same way?

**Structural form.** Portrait gallery — twenty-five figures introduced as a procession, the camera moving down a hall of scrolls; the frame narrative is the sealing of the golden cabinet.

**RPM:** **$8–15.** Psychology/personality classification, high advertiser competition.

**Compliance risk:** **Low–Moderate.** Two distinct exposures. First, typology content attracts "this is pseudoscience" brigading — engagement-positive but brand-safety-negative. Second, and more seriously, the five-form descriptions include complexion terms (green, yellow, red, white, black) that are five-phase colour categories, *not* ethnic descriptors, and will be misread by a Tier-1 audience if not explicitly glossed. **Mandatory:** an on-camera line early in the episode establishing that these are phase-correspondence colours in a symbolic system, and a closing reckoning establishing that no modern evidence supports constitutional typing. Do not cut either for pace.

### 7. Suwen 67 《五运行大论 — Great Discourse on the Five Movements》
**Working title:** *What Holds the Earth Up*

**Summary.** Buried inside a dense treatise on the five movements is one of the most startling exchanges in the canon. The Emperor asks whether the Earth is truly below. Qibo answers: the Earth is beneath humanity, *yet it exists within the great void*. The Emperor asks what supports it. Qibo answers: *it is upheld by the great qi*. Dryness dries it, heat steams it, wind moves it, damp moistens it, cold hardens it, fire warms it. Earlier in the same chapter, Qibo corrects a received authority — Guiyuqu, who held that the Earth is still — by arguing that he observed only the upper signs and could not see the whole.

**Why chosen.** This is the strongest pure history-of-science episode available and it has never been popularised for an English audience. A text usually filed under "traditional medicine" contains a suspended-earth cosmology, an explicit correction of an earlier authority, and an argument from the limits of observation. The comparison set is Anaximander, the Xuanye school, and the long road to universal gravitation — all of which raise the ceiling on this episode considerably.

**Structural form.** Cross-examination — the Emperor refuses each answer and pushes again, four times, until the cosmological claim is forced into the open.

**RPM:** **$8–14.** Astronomy/history-of-science classification, very strong Tier-1 male 25–54 index.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** No medical content in the selected material at all. The one discipline required is honesty: this is *not* a discovery of gravity, and claiming so would invite well-deserved correction. Frame it as a cosmological intuition arrived at by reasoning about qi — remarkable for what it rejects (the Earth resting on something) rather than for what it proves.

### 8. Suwen 77 《疏五过论 — On the Five Faults》
**Working title:** *The Five Ways a Doctor Fails You*

**Summary.** A treatise on malpractice. Before diagnosing, the physician must ask whether the patient was once noble and has fallen; whether once wealthy and is now poor. Such a person may develop illness with no external cause and no organ sign — the text names these conditions *tuoying* and *shijing*. The physician who examines and finds nothing, and therefore concludes there is nothing, has committed the first fault. Four more follow, each a failure of attention rather than technique. Leigong, the student, rises and bows twice and admits he has never heard of any of them.

**Why chosen.** This is a medical ethics text, and it is doing something the Hippocratic Oath does not: it insists that social position, financial loss, and grief are *clinical data*, and that a physician who ignores them is not merely incomplete but at fault. For a Tier-1 audience this maps directly onto live contemporary arguments about social determinants of health and about what medicine misses when it looks only at the body. It is the highest-value episode on the slate for the "this text is smarter than you expect" thesis.

**Structural form.** Tribunal — the episode is staged as a review hearing, each fault introduced as a charge with a case attached.

**RPM:** **$8–14.** Ethics/professional classification. Attracts professional-services and education advertisers; strong LinkedIn-adjacent share behaviour.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** No treatment content. Contains no claim that could contradict health-authority guidance. The one care point: do not extend the argument into criticism of any identifiable contemporary healthcare system, which would reclassify the episode as controversial-issues content.

### 9. Suwen 12 《异法方宜论 — Different Methods, Different Suitabilities》
**Working title:** *Five Regions, Five Medicines*

**Summary.** Why do physicians cure the same illness by different means? Because of terrain. In the east, by the sea, people eat fish and salt and suffer abscesses — so stone needles arose there. In the west, a land of metal and jade, rich food and coarse clothing, illness arises internally — so herbal medicine arose there. In the north, high and cold, people live outdoors and eat dairy, and cold accumulates — so moxibustion arose there. In the south, low and damp, people eat fermented foods and suffer cramps and *bi* — so the fine needles arose there. In the centre, level and moist, with varied diet and little labour — so guided exercise and manipulation arose there. The sage physician combines all five.

**Why chosen.** This is a theory of *why medical traditions differ*, written from inside one of them, around two thousand years before comparative medical anthropology existed. It is also the cleanest possible answer to the audience's likeliest objection — "why should I care about a system that isn't mine?" — because the chapter's own answer is that no system is universal and all five are partial. Structurally it is a gift: five discrete acts, geographically distinct, visually distinct.

**Structural form.** Compass rose — five stations, each a self-contained travelogue segment, converging on the centre.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** History/geography/anthropology classification.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** The chapter makes broad characterological generalisations about regional populations (complexions, skin texture, diet, habits) that read badly to a Tier-1 ear if delivered flat. These must be presented as *what a Han-dynasty writer believed about people in other regions*, with the ethnographic distance made explicit. Handled that way it is an asset — the episode becomes partly about how medical theory encodes prejudice about outsiders.

### 10. Lingshu 50 《论勇 — On Bravery》
**Working title:** *Where Courage Lives*

**Summary.** The Emperor observes that two men of the same age, dressed the same, caught in the same storm, do not both fall ill — and asks why. Shao Yu answers first with constitution, then the Emperor pushes into stranger territory: what makes a man brave? Shao Yu describes the brave man anatomically — deep firm eyes, straight nose, the three burners set horizontally, the heart upright, the liver large and firm, the gallbladder *full and broad*. When angry, his qi fills his chest, his liver rises, his gallbladder spreads, his hair stands on end. The timid man's gallbladder hangs slack and does not fill. Then the sharpest question: why does a timid man who drinks become fearless? Because wine is fierce qi that fills the chest and floats the liver and spreads the gallbladder — *and when the qi declines, regret follows*.

**Why chosen.** A physiological theory of courage, an anatomical theory of cowardice, and an explanation of drunken bravado — with a closing line that is almost unbearably good. This is the most purely entertaining chapter on the slate and it still carries real intellectual freight: courage is located in an organ, which is why "gall" survives in English as a word for nerve. That etymological bridge (gall, galling, gallbladder; *dan* 胆 in Chinese meaning both organ and courage) is a genuine payoff for an English-speaking audience.

**Structural form.** Duel — two subjects tracked in parallel throughout, the brave man and the timid man, in split composition.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** Psychology classification, discounted slightly for the alcohol section.

**Compliance risk:** **Low–Moderate.** Two flags. First, the alcohol material must not glamorise — the chapter's own moral (*regret follows*) does the work, so land on it rather than on the bravado. Second, and more important, the earlier section of this chapter maps disease susceptibility onto complexion colours including "black" and states that dark-skinned people with thick skin are unaffected by seasonal winds. **Out of context this is a racial claim.** In context it is the five-phase colour system applied to constitution. Either gloss it explicitly and carefully, or open the episode at section 3 and skip the constitutional material entirely. The second option is safer and loses nothing.

### 11. Lingshu 31 《肠胃 — Intestines and Stomach》
**Working title:** *They Measured It*

**Summary.** A pure measurement log. From lips to teeth, nine *fen*. The mouth, two *cun* five *fen* wide. The tongue weighs ten *liang*, seven *cun* long. From the pharynx to the stomach, one *chi* six *cun*. The stomach, uncoiled, two *chi* six *cun*, capacity three *dou* five *sheng*. The small intestine coils to the left in overlapping rings. The large intestine has sixteen bends and runs three *zhang* three *chi*. Numbers, all the way down.

**Why chosen.** This is the hardest evidence in the canon that somebody opened a body and measured what was inside — and the numbers are, for the large intestine and the stomach, in a plausible range. Paired with the dissection sentence from Lingshu 12, it dismantles the widespread assumption that classical Chinese medicine was purely speculative. That is a genuinely revisionist argument delivered with a ruler, which is the most persuasive form an argument can take on video.

**Structural form.** Audit — the episode is a measurement survey, tallying as it goes, with a running comparison against modern anatomical figures.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** Anatomy/science/history classification. Numbers-versus-modern-numbers is a high-retention format.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** Purely descriptive anatomy. Keep visuals diagrammatic. **Deliberate exclusion:** the companion chapter Lingshu 32 《平人绝谷》 calculates how many days a person survives without food using these same capacities. It is intellectually continuous and it is **excluded from this slate on purpose** — a video about survival duration without food is an eating-disorder-adjacent artifact regardless of framing, and no amount of historical distance fixes that. Do not fold it in as a coda.

### 12. Suwen 81 《解精微论 — Explanation of Subtle Principles》
**Working title:** *Why We Weep*

**Summary.** Leigong asks the Emperor a question he admits may be foolish and is not covered in the classics: when a person weeps and no tears come, or tears come but little nasal discharge — why? He does not even know where the water comes from. The Emperor's first reply is remarkable: *this will be of no benefit to treatment. It is knowledge a skilled physician possesses, and it arises from the Dao.* He then answers anyway — the heart as master of the essences, the eye as their gate, water and fire in contention, grief driving the spirit outward and the fluid following.

**Why chosen.** A chapter about crying, framed by an explicit admission that the knowledge is useless — which is precisely the definition of curiosity-driven inquiry, and precisely what makes it the emotional close of the series. It is also the final chapter of the Suwen, which gives any episode built on it a natural terminal weight. Short, self-contained, and unexpectedly moving.

**Structural form.** Chamber piece — two characters, one room, one question, spiralling inward. The tightest form on the slate, and the deliberate contrast against the sprawling episodes around it.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** Psychology/physiology classification.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** No treatment, no condition. The modern reckoning (basal, reflex, and emotional tears; the unresolved question of why humans alone weep from emotion) is genuinely interesting and strengthens the episode.

### 13. Suwen 26 《八正神明论 — The Eight Cardinal Directions and Divine Clarity》
**Working title:** *The Moon in the Blood*

**Summary.** Needling must wait upon the qi of sun, moon, stars, seasons and the eight directions. On warm bright days the blood runs thin and defensive qi rises to the surface; on cold overcast days blood is stagnant and viscous and defensive qi sinks. As the moon waxes, blood and qi refine and begin to circulate; at full moon they are abundant and the flesh is firm; as the moon wanes the channels empty and the body stands alone. Therefore: do not drain at the new moon, do not tonify at the full, do not treat at the dark of the moon.

**Why chosen.** A systematic claim that human physiology tracks the lunar cycle — stated as operational doctrine, with prohibitions attached. This is the episode where the series can do its most valuable work: take a claim seriously, describe it fully, and then examine it honestly against the modern literature, which is genuinely mixed and mostly negative. The comparison to the persistent modern belief in lunar effects on behaviour (documented, and documented as unsupported) gives the episode a live contemporary edge.

**Structural form.** Almanac — the episode runs one full lunation, waxing to waning, with the doctrine introduced phase by phase.

**RPM:** **$6–12.** Astronomy-adjacent, discounted for pseudoscience proximity.

**Compliance risk:** **Low–Moderate.** This is the highest medical-misinformation exposure on the slate, because it is a physiological claim with a treatment prohibition attached. It is also entirely manageable: the reckoning is not optional here, it is the episode's spine. State plainly that controlled studies have not supported lunar effects on human physiology or behaviour. If that reckoning is cut for pace, this episode should not ship.

### 14. Lingshu 77 《九宫八风 — Nine Palaces and Eight Winds》
**Working title:** *The Magic Square of the Winds*

**Summary.** Taiyi moves through nine palaces on a fixed calendar, forty-six days in each, beginning at the winter solstice and returning. Wind and rain on the day of the move are auspicious — the year will be prosperous, the people at peace, disease rare. Early arrival brings excessive rain, late arrival brings heat. A change at the solstice is an omen concerning the ruler; at the equinox, the prime minister; in the central palace, the officials; at the autumn equinox, the generals; at the summer solstice, the common people.

**Why chosen.** The nine-palace grid is the *Luoshu* magic square — the oldest known magic square, in which every row, column, and diagonal sums to fifteen. This chapter is therefore a direct link between the medical canon and the history of mathematics, and it lets the episode do something none of the others can: a genuine mathematical demonstration on screen. It is also the clearest case in the canon of medicine, meteorology, calendar, and state divination as a single undifferentiated field — which is a better answer to "was this science?" than any argument the narrator could make.

**Structural form.** Grid traversal — the episode walks the nine-palace square in sequence, the magic-square property revealed as the walk completes.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** Mathematics/history-of-science classification. Magic-square content has strong evergreen search behaviour.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** Effectively zero medical content. The omen material is state divination, treated historically. The one care point is not to present the astrological omens as predictive, which the mathematical framing naturally avoids.

### 15. Lingshu 54 《天年 — Heavenly Years》
**Working title:** *The Ten Ages of a Life*

**Summary.** The Emperor asks what a person is founded on at birth. The mother's essence is the foundation, the father's the shield; those who keep *shen* live, those who lose it die. Then the decade ladder: at ten the five zang are settled and the person loves to run; at twenty blood and qi begin to flourish and the person loves to walk fast; at thirty the person loves to sit; at forty to lie down — and so on, decade by decade, faculty by faculty, to a hundred, when the five zang are all empty, the spirit qi departs, and the form alone remains.

**Why chosen.** A structurally perfect episode — ten acts, one per decade, with an inbuilt emotional arc that requires no invention. The comparison to Shakespeare's seven ages and to the medieval ages-of-man tradition places it in a lineage a Tier-1 audience already recognises, and the specificity ("loves to run," "loves to sit," "loves to lie down") is more affecting than any abstraction.

**Structural form.** Decade ladder — ten fixed-length acts, each visually and musically identical in shape, accumulating weight through repetition.

**RPM:** **$6–12.** Discounted from its natural band because ageing and lifespan titles carry keyword-level drag with some advertisers, and because the Tier-1 algorithm reads "longevity" as wellness. **Mitigation is title discipline:** *The Ten Ages of a Life* or *A Life in Ten Decades*, never *longevity*, never *live to 100*.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** No advice, no protocol. Avoid any implication that the described trajectory is achievable or avoidable through behaviour, which would convert it into wellness content and drop it a full RPM band.

### 16. Suwen 9 《六节藏象论 — The Six Periods》
**Working title:** *The 365-Day Body*

**Summary.** Heaven completes a year by sixes; humans are ordered by nines. The sun moves one degree a day, the moon thirteen degrees and a fraction, and so a year of alternating long and short months comes to 365 days, with the accumulated surplus resolved by an intercalary month. The human body has 365 joints, answering to the days. From this arithmetic the chapter derives the six periods, the seasonal governance of the organs, and the correspondence system that organises the rest of the canon.

**Why chosen.** The calendar came first. This chapter shows the medical system being *derived from* astronomical bookkeeping — the intercalary month, the 13-degree lunar motion, the reconciliation of solar and lunar years. That is a real and difficult piece of ancient science, and it explains why the body was described in the terms it was. It is the strongest available answer to "where did all these numbers come from?"

**Structural form.** Clockwork — the episode is built as a mechanism assembling, gear by gear, from solar day to intercalary correction to the body's 365 joints.

**RPM:** **$7–12.** Astronomy/calendar/history classification. Denser than the slate average, so retention risk is the constraint rather than category.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** Astronomical and arithmetical. The 365-joints correspondence should be presented as numerological reasoning, not anatomy — which is honest and also more interesting.

### 17. Lingshu 71 《邪客 — The Guest Pathogen》 *(section 3 only)*
**Working title:** *A Body Shaped Like the World*

**Summary.** The Emperor asks how the limbs and joints correspond to heaven and earth, and Bo Gao delivers the most complete microcosm catalogue in the canon. Heaven is round and earth square, so the head is round and the feet square. Heaven has sun and moon; we have two eyes. Earth has nine provinces; we have nine orifices. Heaven has wind and rain; we have joy and anger. Thunder and lightning; voice and sound. Four seasons; four limbs. Twelve great rivers; twelve channels. Springs and underground channels; defensive qi. Grasses and herbs; body hair. Mountains and rocks; the prominent bones. A year of 365 days; a body of 360 joints.

**Why chosen.** The macrocosm-microcosm idea is one of the genuinely universal structures in human thought — Vitruvian man, the Kabbalistic Adam Kadmon, the Norse Ymir, the Puruṣa of the Ṛgveda. This is its most systematic Chinese statement, and it lets the episode be comparative in a way that lands hard with a Tier-1 audience. It is also the most visually generative chapter on the slate: every line is a split-screen.

**Structural form.** Mirror — the frame is split for the entire runtime, world on one side, body on the other, the correspondences accumulating until the final line breaks the symmetry.

**RPM:** **$6–12.** Comparative philosophy/cosmology classification.

**Compliance risk:** **Low.** Requires editing discipline: **use section 3 only.** Sections 1–2 of this chapter cover insomnia and prescribe a wine-based decoction with dosage by the cup — that is a treatment protocol and must not appear. The correspondence catalogue also contains an anatomical line about the penis and a statement that women "lack two segments"; handle the sex-difference material briefly and factually as a historical view, or excise it. Excision is cleaner.

### 18. Suwen 75 《著至教论 — On Establishing Authoritative Teachings》
**Working title:** *What a Physician Was Supposed to Know*

**Summary.** The Emperor asks Leigong whether he knows the way of medicine. Leigong answers, carefully, that he can recite it — enough to treat officials, not enough to treat kings. He asks to receive the principles that harmonise heavenly measure with the four seasons, that distinguish the stars from the light of sun and moon. The Emperor's answer sets the standard: one who understands this *will know astronomy above, geography below, and human affairs in the middle*, and such knowledge can be taught to the common people without doubt or danger.

**Why chosen.** This is the ancient job description, and it is not a medical one — it is astronomy, geography, and human affairs, held together. For a Tier-1 audience this is an unusually live topic: it is the case for the generalist, made two thousand years ago, in a field that has since specialised beyond recognition. The Leigong framing (a student admitting his competence has a ceiling) is honest and unusually humane.

**Structural form.** Viva voce — the episode is an oral examination, the student tested in real time, with the audience implicitly sitting the same exam.

**RPM:** **$7–13.** Education/pedagogy/professional classification.

**Compliance risk:** **Very Low.** No clinical content whatever. This is the safest chapter on the entire slate.

### 19. Lingshu 46 《五变 — Five Transformations》
**Working title:** *The Grain of the Wood*

**Summary.** Why do people caught in the same wind not fall ill alike? Shao Yu first corrects the premise: heaven's wind is not aimed at anyone. It moves fairly and uprightly; those exposed to it fall ill and those who avoid it do not. *It does not seek out people — people expose themselves to it.* Then the analogy that carries the chapter: a carpenter sharpens his axes and chisels and cuts wood. Even within one tree, hard and soft differ; the hard will not cut, the soft splits and flakes, and at the knots the tools themselves break. So with bodies.

**Why chosen.** This is a theory of individual susceptibility — why identical exposure produces different outcomes — arrived at through a workshop analogy of unusual precision. The carpenter's bench is a better visual than anything the series could invent, and the underlying question (why did that person get sick and I didn't?) is one every viewer has asked. The line about wind not seeking anyone out is also an early and clean statement against the moralisation of illness.

**Structural form.** Workshop parable — the episode is set at the carpenter's bench, the argument built by cutting wood, the medical claims arriving as consequences.

**RPM:** **$6–11.** History/science classification, discounted for disease-susceptibility adjacency.

**Compliance risk:** **Low–Moderate.** The subject is why people fall ill, which sits closer to the medical line than most of this slate. Manageable with strict historical framing and no modern comparison to any specific disease or exposure. Do not gesture at contemporary epidemiology or immunity — that reclassifies the episode instantly.

### 20. Suwen 39 《举痛论 — On Pain》
**Working title:** *Nine Ways the Body Moves*

**Summary.** The chapter opens with a statement of method that could serve as the series' epigraph: *one who speaks well of Heaven must demonstrate proof in human affairs; one who speaks well of the past must find relevance in the present; one who speaks well of others must have insight into himself.* The Emperor then demands an account of pain that can be seen, touched, and personally verified — fourteen distinct presentations, each traced to cold qi lodging in a different place. The chapter closes with its most quoted passage: the hundred diseases arise from qi. Anger sends qi up; joy relaxes it; grief consumes it; fear sends it down; cold contracts it; heat opens it; fright disorders it; exertion exhausts it; thought knots it.

**Why chosen.** The closing passage is the oldest systematic psychosomatic model in the canon — nine emotional and physical states, each with a specific and *directional* effect on the body. Anger moving upward and thought knotting are metaphors precise enough to still feel accurate. The opening epistemological statement is separately valuable and worth foregrounding.

**Structural form.** Deposition — fourteen presentations recorded as testimony, resolving into a single law in the final act.

**RPM:** **$5–10.** Lowest band on the slate. Pain is a symptom category and the classifier will read it that way regardless of framing, which caps the ceiling.

**Compliance risk:** **Moderate** — the highest on the slate. Fourteen pain presentations with attributed causes is structurally indistinguishable from a diagnostic guide. **Mitigations, all required:** lead with the epistemology, not the symptoms; compress the fourteen presentations into a montage rather than an enumerated list; never attach a treatment to any presentation; place the nine-motions-of-qi passage as the episode's true subject and the pain material as its setup. If the episode cannot be built that way, drop it and promote Suwen 78 from the bench.

## 5. Publish sequence

Four phases, twenty slots. The sequence is optimised for **sustained** engagement, which is a different objective from maximum launch velocity — it deliberately holds back three top-decile episodes to arrest the retention decay that normally begins around episode 6.

### Phase 1 — Establish (Slots 1–5)
Prove the format, define the channel's category to the algorithm, and win the first thousand subscribers on curiosity rather than on subject-matter loyalty.

| Slot | Episode | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lingshu 28 — Why We Yawn** | Lowest barrier to entry in the entire canon. Requires no prior interest in China, medicine, or history. Zero compliance surface for the channel's first algorithmic read. |
| 2 | **Suwen 13 — When Medicine Stopped Being Magic** | The thesis statement. Establishes immediately that this is intellectual history, not wellness — which is the single most important signal to send in the first month, because early classification is sticky. |
| 3 | **Lingshu 8 — The Ancient Map of the Mind** | Highest RPM band on the slate, deployed early to establish the revenue floor while impressions are cheap. Also the strongest "this text is smarter than you assumed" episode. |
| 4 | **Suwen 67 — What Holds the Earth Up** | Hard pivot into astronomy. Proves the channel is not a single-format operation, which matters for both the algorithm and for Generic/Repetitive Content exposure. |
| 5 | **Lingshu 43 — The Dream Catalogue** | Highest comment-generation potential. Placed at slot 5 to seed community activity just as the first cohort of subscribers arrives. |

### Phase 2 — Widen (Slots 6–10)
Broaden the category footprint, test the ethics and anatomy pillars, and place the first deliberately-held banger.

| Slot | Episode | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | **Suwen 77 — The Five Ways a Doctor Fails You** | Tests the ethics pillar. Highest share-to-professional-networks potential; different audience than slots 1–5, which widens the recommendation graph. |
| 7 | **Lingshu 12 — The Rivers Inside** | First anatomy episode. The dissection reveal is the strongest single mid-video hook available and is placed where retention curves typically first sag. |
| 8 | **Lingshu 50 — Where Courage Lives** | **Held banger #1.** Rank-10 by composite but top-3 by pure entertainment. Deployed to arrest the episode-8 decay that kills most documentary channels. |
| 9 | **Suwen 12 — Five Regions, Five Medicines** | Answers the objection the audience has by now formed ("why should this apply to me?"). Best placed after the audience is invested enough to have the objection. |
| 10 | **Lingshu 77 — The Magic Square of the Winds** | Mathematics pillar debut. Strong evergreen search tail — the first episode on the slate likely to still be acquiring impressions in year two. |

### Phase 3 — Deepen (Slots 11–15)
The audience now exists. Chapters that need context, or that reward a viewer who has seen five episodes, become viable.

| Slot | Episode | Why here |

| 11 | **Lingshu 64 — Twenty-Five Kinds of Person** | **Held banger #2.** Highest engagement of the psychology pillar, but the highest brigading risk — deliberately held until the comment community is established enough to self-moderate. |

| 15 | **Lingshu 71 §3 — A Body Shaped Like the World** | Visually the most spectacular. Placed as a Phase 3 payoff and as the natural setup for the ages-of-man episode following. |

### Phase 4 — Consolidate (Slots 16–20)
Longer-tail, connoisseur, and constrained episodes, with the emotional close reserved for last.

| Slot | Episode | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | **Lingshu 54 — The Ten Ages of a Life** | Emotional register shift. The decade-ladder form is the most distinctive on the slate and reads as an event rather than an instalment. |

### 5.1 Calendar overlay

Slot cadence assumed at one episode per fortnight (~10 months for the slate). Overlay the seasonality multiplier:

- **Push Slots 3, 6, 8, 11 into October–December** wherever the cadence permits. These are the four highest-RPM episodes and Q4 is worth 1.4–1.8×. Moving one $9–16 episode from January to November is worth more than the entire RPM difference between the best and worst chapter on this list.
- **Place Slots 12, 16, 18, 19 in January–February.** These are the lowest-band episodes; spending them in the trough costs least.
- Do not reorder to chase this if it breaks the Phase logic. Sequence integrity beats a single quarter's multiplier.

## 6. Where I'd push back

**The premise that highest RPM and lowest compliance risk point the same way is only mostly true.** They align for fourteen of these twenty. They diverge for the other six — the psychology and typology episodes (Lingshu 64, 50, 43; Suwen 26, 39) are simultaneously the highest-engagement *and* the most pseudoscience-adjacent material on the slate. The slate above resolves this in favour of compliance, which costs an estimated $0.80–1.40 in weighted RPM. That is the right trade, but it is a trade, and it should be made knowingly rather than discovered later.

**Chapter selection is probably not the binding constraint.** Twenty episodes at 18:30+ is roughly seven hours of scripted, storyboarded, voiced, and rendered output. At the current package specification that is a substantial multi-quarter commitment. If throughput is the real constraint, the higher-leverage question is not *which twenty chapters* but *which six*, produced to a higher standard, with the rest held. A slate of twenty mediocre episodes underperforms six excellent ones on every metric including RPM, because RPM is downstream of watch time.

**RPM is a second-order lever.** Topic-driven RPM variation across this slate spans roughly $5–16 — about 3×. Watch-time-driven variation on a single episode spans considerably more than that, because retention affects impressions, impressions affect the recommendation surface, and the recommendation surface affects audience geography, which is itself the largest RPM input. Optimising chapter selection for RPM is worth doing, but it is worth roughly a third of what optimising the first ninety seconds is worth.

**The synthetic-content disclosure is the most under-weighted risk in the project.** Three AI-generated characters delivering historical and physiological content is precisely the configuration the disclosure requirement exists for. The exposure is low because the animation is stylised and the characters are transparently fictional — but the cost of compliance is a checkbox and one line of description text, and the cost of non-compliance is a monetisation review. Disclose on every episode from slot 1.

**One argument against the held-banger structure.** Front-loading the five strongest episodes would maximise launch velocity, and there is a real case that early algorithmic momentum compounds more than late-slate retention does. The sequence above rejects that in favour of a sawtooth, on the grounds that a visibly declining quality curve reads as channel decay and is harder to recover from than a slow start. This is a genuine judgement call, not a settled one — if the pilot's first three episodes underperform on impressions rather than on retention, invert it and spend slots 8, 11 and 20 immediately.
